```
Seneca wrote a whole book against anger: whatever good it seems to do, reason can do better. Aristotle disagreed: being angry at the right person, to the right degree, at the right time and for the right reason is a virtue, and never getting angry at real wrongs is a failing.
```

**Caption**

```
Anger has two bad scripts: let it out, or keep it in. Venting rehearses it. Suppression hides it without resolving it.

The third way is to treat anger as information: something you value was blocked, crossed or threatened. Find out what, and the response gets clearer.

Save this for after the next argument. Not during.
```

## 6. Three Substack Notes (aim 250–300 characters; embed the post, don't link out)

1. Most negative emotions pull us away. Anger is unusual: research links it to approach, the drive to push toward a blocked goal. That's why it feels like strength, and why the useful question is never 'how do I get rid of it?' but 'what did it want?' *(248 chars)*
2. In 2002, students who hit a punching bag while thinking about the person who'd insulted them came out angrier and more aggressive than students who simply sat quietly. Catharsis is one of the most repeated ideas in pop psychology. In that test, it made things worse. *(266 chars)*
3. Aristotle: "anyone can get angry — that is easy." The hard part, he said, is the right person, the right amount, the right time, the right reason. Seneca thought even that was too generous to anger. Both would agree most of ours is the wrong size. *(247 chars)*
