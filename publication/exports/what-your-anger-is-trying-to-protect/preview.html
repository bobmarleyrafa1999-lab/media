What Your Anger Is Trying to Protect

Anger moves us toward things, not away from them. Venting doesn't drain it and swallowing it doesn't either. The more useful question is what it's standing in front of.

Anger has a strange reputation. Among emotions it’s the one we’re most often told to manage, release, control, let go of. Men in particular are given two scripts for it: let it out or keep it in. Hit something, or say nothing and carry it home.

Both scripts treat anger as a kind of pressure that has to go somewhere. The research suggests that picture is wrong in a useful way. Anger isn’t steam. It’s closer to a guard dog: it gets up and moves when something it cares about is threatened. If you want it to settle, it helps to know what it’s guarding.

An emotion that moves toward

Most negative emotions pull us back. Fear makes us freeze or flee. Sadness makes us withdraw. Anger is unusual. In a 2009 review, the psychologists Charles Carver and Eddie Harmon-Jones gathered evidence that anger is an approach-related emotion: it tends to arise when a goal is blocked, and it energises us to push toward the goal or the obstacle rather than away from it. [Review: Carver & Harmon-Jones, 2009]

That fits ordinary experience. You get angry when something you want is in the way: a fair hearing, respect, safety, a plan, the version of yourself you were trying to be today. Anger’s job is to remove the obstacle. That’s why it feels so much like strength, and why, misdirected, it does so much damage.

It also explains why the question “what is my anger protecting?” tends to have an answer. If anger is the emotion of a blocked goal, there’s always a goal.

Why venting doesn’t work

The old “let it out” script has a name in psychology: catharsis. The idea, going back to Freud and well beyond, is that expressing anger drains it.

In 2002 Brad Bushman tested the idea directly. He had 600 university students write an essay, then deliberately angered them with a harsh, insulting critique from a supposed partner. Some then hit a punching bag while thinking about the person who’d insulted them. Some hit the bag while thinking about getting fit. A third group simply sat quietly for a few minutes. Afterwards, everyone had a chance to blast their critic with unpleasant noise.

The people who vented while thinking about their critic reported the most anger and were the most aggressive. Sitting quietly beat venting. [Experiment: Bushman, 2002] Hitting things while rehearsing the grievance didn’t empty the tank. It filled it.

Why swallowing it doesn’t work either

The other script, keep it in, fares no better over time. Research on emotion regulation by James Gross and Oliver John found that people who habitually suppress the outward expression of their emotions tend to report more negative emotion, lower wellbeing and weaker social closeness than people who habitually reappraise, that is, change how they interpret what’s happening. [Study: Gross & John, 2003] The studies are correlational, and suppression has its uses in the moment (a funeral, a meeting, a child watching). But as a way of life, it seems to cost more than it saves.

So the two scripts men are handed both fail. Venting rehearses the anger. Suppression hides it without resolving it. What’s left?

The third option: name it, then ask what it’s for

The same research that lets us down on venting points somewhere better. In Matthew Lieberman’s 2007 brain-imaging work, people who simply put a label on an emotion in a picture (“angry”) showed reduced activity in the amygdala compared with other ways of processing the image. [Study: Lieberman et al., 2007] It’s a lab result, not a technique for real fury. But it suggests that turning anger into language, rather than into action or silence, starts to change it.

The second step is the question in this essay’s title. Some therapists describe anger as often being a “secondary” emotion that sits on top of something more exposed: hurt, fear, shame, grief. [Clinical framework, not a tested law] Sometimes that’s exactly right. The man shouting about the dishes is, underneath, frightened that he doesn’t matter in his own house. But sometimes anger is simply, accurately, about the thing itself: a promise broken, a line crossed, an injustice. You don’t need to dig for a hidden wound every time. You only need to ask the guard dog what it’s standing in front of.

The answers tend to fall into a few kinds: - A boundary. Something was taken or crossed: time, body, space, trust. - A value. Something you believe in was violated: fairness, honesty, loyalty. - A standing. Your place was threatened: respect, competence, being taken seriously. - A softer feeling. Hurt, fear, embarrassment or loss that anger is easier to feel than.

Each answer points to a different response. A crossed boundary calls for a clear sentence. A violated value might call for action. A threatened standing often calls for patience, because the threat is smaller than it feels. A softer feeling usually needs to be said, out loud, to the person who could hear it.

Two philosophers who disagreed

The ancient world argued about anger more than about almost any other emotion, and the argument is still useful.

Seneca wrote a whole treatise against it. In On Anger he reports that some wise men have called anger a brief madness, and he argues that it’s never needed: whatever good anger seems to do, reason can do better and without the damage. [Philosophy: Seneca, On Anger I, paraphrased] For Seneca, the goal is not well-managed anger but freedom from it.

Aristotle thought that went too far. In the Nicomachean Ethics he treats a good temper as a virtue lying between irascibility and a kind of spinelessness, and he puts the difficulty plainly: “anyone can get angry — that is easy.” What’s hard, he goes on, is to be angry with the right person, to the right degree, at the right time, for the right reason and in the right way. [Philosophy: Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics II.9, tr. Ross; first clause quoted, rest paraphrased] For Aristotle, a man who never gets angry at real wrongs is missing something.

You don’t have to settle the argument to use it. Seneca is right that most of our anger, in the moment, is disproportionate and better replaced by clear thinking. Aristotle is right that some anger is the correct response to something that matters. The question “what is this protecting?” serves both: it cools what’s out of proportion and sharpens what isn’t. [Moodius reading]

Try this: The Anger Audit (10 minutes, after the heat has passed)

Don’t do this mid-argument. Wait until your heart rate is back to normal. 1. What happened? Write it as a camera would record it. No adjectives. 2. Name it. Write the emotion with the most precise word you can: irritated, insulted, betrayed, humiliated, furious. 3. What was it guarding? Pick one: a boundary, a value, a standing, a softer feeling. Write what exactly. 4. Was the size right? On a scale of 1–10, how big was the threat, and how big was the anger? 5. What does the thing it was guarding actually need? One sentence to say, one action to take, or nothing at all.

Three questions for your journal

  1. What did I get angry about this week, and what was the anger standing in front of?
  2. Which feeling do I find it easier to turn into anger than to say out loud?
  3. When was the last time my anger was exactly right, and what did it protect?

Listening to the guard

Anger is not your enemy, and it isn’t your strength either. It’s information arriving with a lot of energy attached. Venting spends the energy and throws the information away. Swallowing it keeps the energy and loses the information. Naming it and asking what it’s guarding keeps the information and lets the energy go where it’s actually needed.

Seneca wanted to be free of anger; Aristotle wanted to use it well. Most of us, most of the time, would settle for understanding it, which turns out to be the first step toward both.

If your anger frightens you or the people around you, or you’ve hurt someone when angry, please speak to a GP or look for an anger-management or men’s support service near you. That is a strong thing to do, not a weak one.
