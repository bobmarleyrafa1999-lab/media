The Exhaustion of Always Trying to Become Someone

There is a tiredness that sleep doesn't fix. It comes from treating yourself as a permanent renovation project, and it's worth understanding before it turns into something worse.

There’s a kind of tiredness that doesn’t come from doing too much. You can feel it on a quiet Sunday with nothing scheduled. It’s the tiredness of being, at all times, in progress.

You are learning a language, fixing your posture, building a side project, reading the right books, working on your attachment style, improving your sleep, becoming more disciplined, more present, more yourself. Each item on its own is reasonable. Together, they add up to a life in which you are never allowed to simply be someone. You are always on the way to becoming someone else.

The self as a project

The philosopher Byung-Chul Han argues that we’ve moved from a society of prohibition, where people were told what they couldn’t do, to a society of achievement, where we’re told we can do anything, and so must. In his account the result isn’t freedom but a new kind of exhaustion: people who exploit themselves, voluntarily, in the name of self-realisation, and burn out without anyone forcing them to. [Idea: Han, The Burnout Society, 2010; paraphrased]

It’s a provocative thesis, not a finding. But it describes something familiar. Nobody is making you optimise your mornings. That’s what makes it so hard to stop.

The gap that never closes

Psychology offers a more measured way of describing the same strain. In 1987 Tory Higgins proposed self-discrepancy theory. We hold several pictures of ourselves: the actual self, who we think we are; the ideal self, who we’d like to be; and the ought self, who we think we should be. Gaps between the actual and the ideal, he suggested, bring dejection: disappointment, dissatisfaction. Gaps between the actual and the ought bring agitation: guilt, anxiety, unease. [Theory: Higgins, 1987] Later studies have backed the general link between these gaps and distress, while questioning whether each kind of gap maps neatly onto one kind of feeling.

Now picture a self that’s always under construction. The ideal keeps moving, because every improvement reveals the next thing to improve. The gap never closes. If Higgins is even roughly right, then a life organised around perpetual becoming is a life organised around a permanent, low-level discomfort.

There’s evidence the pressure is rising. Between 1989 and 2016, measured perfectionism among students in the US, Canada and the UK went up, and the steepest climb, about a third, was in the belief that other people expect you to be perfect. [Meta-analysis: Curran & Hill, 2019]

Not quite burnout

It’s worth being careful with words here. The World Health Organization describes burn-out as an occupational phenomenon, not a medical condition: the result of chronic workplace stress that hasn’t been successfully managed, with three features, exhaustion, increasing mental distance from or cynicism about one’s job, and reduced professional efficacy. [Clinical framework: WHO, ICD-11] The WHO is explicit that the term should be kept for work.

What this essay describes is related but wider. It’s what happens when the logic of a demanding job spreads to the whole self: when you become your own manager, with targets, reviews and performance improvement plans for your personality. It has burnout’s flavour, the exhaustion, the cynicism about the next self-help thing, the sense that none of it is working, without a single employer to blame. [Moodius reading]

An ancient restlessness

The Stoics knew a version of this. In On Tranquillity of Mind, Seneca describes people who are never content with themselves: forever starting new plans and dropping them, travelling from place to place, changing one pursuit for another, and finding that the restlessness travels with them, because what they are trying to escape is themselves. [Philosophy: Seneca, On Tranquillity of Mind 2, paraphrased]

His prescription wasn’t more effort. It was a steadier relationship with the self you already are: choosing a few things worth doing well, and letting go of the constant search for a better version elsewhere.

Growth versus becoming

None of this is an argument against growth. People do change, and wanting to is healthy. The difference is in what the change is for.

Growth is a direction. You get better at something because you care about it, and you can enjoy where you are while still moving.

Becoming, in the exhausting sense, is a verdict. The current self is a draft that isn’t acceptable yet. Every improvement is a down payment on a future self who will finally be enough, and who never arrives, because the standard moves as fast as you do.

From outside, the two can look identical: the same books, the same early alarm, the same plans. The difference shows up in how a bad week feels. A useful test: if you stopped improving tomorrow, would you be allowed to rest? If the honest answer is no, the project has stopped being growth.

Where the pressure comes from

It’s tempting to blame individuals for this, as if the exhausted improver simply lacks perspective. But the invitation to keep becoming is everywhere. Feeds show other people’s progress in real time. Apps score your sleep, your steps, your focus. Work increasingly asks for a “personal brand.” Even rest gets reframed as recovery, a way of performing better tomorrow.

None of that is a conspiracy. It’s simply that a self which is never finished is a self that’s always in the market for the next improvement. That’s worth noticing, not because it lets you off the hook, but because it explains why stopping feels so strange. You’re not just changing a habit. You’re stepping slightly outside a culture that keeps asking who you’ll be next. [Moodius reading]

Letting some of you be finished

The way out isn’t to abandon every goal. It’s to declare parts of yourself finished, not perfect, finished. Your taste in music. Your build. Your personality type. Your morning routine. Done. Good enough to live in.

Finishing things frees up attention for the few areas where you genuinely want to grow. And it lets you meet the people in your life as someone, not as a work in progress who’ll be ready for them later. [Moodius reading]

Try this: The Finished List (15 minutes)

  1. List every area of yourself you’re currently trying to improve. Be thorough; most people find ten or more.
  2. Next to each, write growth (I enjoy getting better at this) or verdict (I feel I’m not acceptable until it’s fixed).
  3. Choose no more than two growth items to keep working on this season.
  4. Write “finished, for now” next to everything else. Unsubscribe, delete the app, put the book back.
  5. In a month, notice whether you miss any of them. Bring back only the ones you genuinely miss.

Three questions for your journal

  1. Who am I trying to become, and what would I have to believe about who I am now to stop?
  2. Which of my self-improvement projects would I keep if nobody ever saw the results?
  3. What part of me could I declare finished this week?

Somewhere to stand

There’s a sentence that every exhausted improver eventually needs to hear: you’re allowed to be someone already. Not a draft. Not a before photo. Someone, with a shape and a history and things you’re good at and things you never will be.

Growth needs somewhere to grow from. If the whole self is always being demolished and rebuilt, there’s nowhere to stand. Finish some of it. Live in it. Then, from there, choose the one or two things worth becoming.

If the exhaustion doesn’t lift with rest, or comes with low mood, hopelessness or thoughts of not wanting to be here, please speak to a GP. In the UK you can call Samaritans on 116 123, any hour. Burnout and depression are real, treatable and not a personal failing.
