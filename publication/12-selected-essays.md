## Theses

- **You Can Understand Yourself and Still Repeat the Same Mistakes.** Understanding yourself is necessary but rarely enough, because much of what we repeat isn't decided in the moment we understand. It runs on habit, cued by context, often while the mind is elsewhere. Change has to be built into the situations where the behaviour happens, not only into the story we tell about it.
- **What Your Anger Is Trying to Protect.** Anger is an approach emotion: it mobilises us toward a goal, a boundary or a wrong to be righted. It usually protects something we value, sometimes a principle, sometimes a more vulnerable feeling. Neither venting nor suppression handles it well. Asking what it's protecting turns anger from a reaction into information.
- **Why Being Needed Can Feel Like Being Loved.** Being needed delivers a dependable version of what we want from love: closeness, purpose, proof that we matter. For people who doubt they'd be loved simply for who they are, usefulness can become the way they earn a place. Psychologists call one version of this *unmitigated communion*: caring for others to the exclusion of the self. It isn't the giving that's the problem. It's the self-neglect and the difficulty receiving that come with it, which quietly keep others from loving the giver back.
- **The Things We Inherit Without Realising.** Everyone inherits ways of being, not just traits: how a household handled conflict, affection, money, illness, failure. Most of it arrives before we can examine it, so it feels like personality rather than a habit. The best-studied versions (maltreatment and attachment) show transmission is real but far from fixed, and the people who break a pattern tend to share a few things: someone who supported them, a coherent account of what happened, and a decision.
- **A Life That Looks Successful but Feels Empty.** A life can look successful and feel empty because we evaluate lives by their most visible features, which is a well-documented error, and because some goals, even when fully achieved, don't feed what people need. The emptiness isn't ingratitude. It's information about which goals were pursued.
- **When Self-Improvement Becomes Self-Rejection.** Self-improvement can be driven by care or by contempt, and from outside they look the same: the gym, the books, the routines. When it's driven by contempt, every plan becomes a verdict on whether you're acceptable, and the research suggests self-criticism predicts less progress, not more. Self-compassion isn't the opposite of high standards. It's what lets people keep them.
- **The Lost Art of Being Alone With Your Thoughts.** Most of us have lost the habit of being alone with our minds, and the first minutes of it are genuinely uncomfortable. But the discomfort is the start, not the verdict: research on short, chosen, device-free solitude suggests it settles strong emotions and makes room for calm. The skill isn't emptying your mind. It's choosing where to point it.
- **The Man You Become When Nobody Is Watching.** Identity is built from private repetition. We become what we repeatedly do (Aristotle), we partly learn who we are by watching what we do (self-perception), and we're capable of fooling ourselves even while we act (Quattrone & Tversky). So the unobserved moments aren't a break from character: they're where most of it is made, and where we gather most of the evidence about who we are.
- **The Parts of Yourself You Were Taught to Hide.** Children learn early which feelings and traits earn approval and which bring trouble, and they hide the second kind. In adulthood the hiding becomes automatic, but it isn't free: research on suppression and on secrets suggests the cost is less in the moment of hiding than in the mental effort of carrying what's hidden. Recovering those parts doesn't mean expressing everything. It means knowing what's there, so you can choose.
- **The Exhaustion of Always Trying to Become Someone.** Modern life invites people to treat the self as a project that's never finished: always optimising, always becoming. Self-discrepancy research suggests the gap between who we are and who we think we should be is a source of distress in itself, and a self kept permanently "under construction" never closes that gap. The remedy isn't giving up on growth. It's letting some of the self be finished, allowed to simply exist.
- **Why We Mistake Intensity for Intimacy.** Intensity (the rush, the longing, the drama, the not-knowing) can feel like the deepest kind of connection, but much of it is arousal and uncertainty rather than closeness. Intimacy, in the research sense, is built from something quieter: gradual, reciprocal disclosure met with responsiveness. People whose early relationships were unpredictable may be especially likely to read the first as the second.
- **What Would You Do If Nobody Was Watching?.** "What would you do if nobody was watching?" is usually asked as a test of virtue. It's more useful as a decision tool. Being watched does change some choices, though less reliably than headlines claimed. The real power of the question is that it strips out reputation and leaves what you actually want, and the research on regret suggests those unwatched wants, the ideal-self ones, are the ones we most regret not acting on.
