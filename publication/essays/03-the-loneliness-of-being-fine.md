# The Loneliness of Being Fine

*Many men aren't lonely in the way the headlines describe. They're fine, and nobody would know if they weren't. The research suggests why, and what it takes to change.*

There is a question that works better than any loneliness questionnaire. It goes like this: *if you were not okay, who would know?*

Not who would care. Most people have someone who would care. Who would actually *know*: notice the change in your voice, ask a second time when you said you were fine, be the person you'd call at eleven at night without rehearsing what to say.

For a lot of men, the honest answer is a short list. Sometimes it's a partner. Sometimes it's nobody. And most of those men would not describe themselves as lonely. They have colleagues, a group chat, people to watch the match with. They're fine.

That gap, between being fine and being known, is what this essay is about.

## What has changed

In 2021 the Survey Center on American Life asked just over two thousand US adults about their friendships. Fifteen percent of men said they had no close friends at all. In a comparable survey in 1990, the figure was three percent. Over the same period, the share of men with six or more close friends fell from 55 percent to 27 percent. **[Survey: Cox, Survey Center on American Life, 2021]**

The usual caveats apply. It's one country, the 1990 figure comes from a different survey, and "close friend" means different things to different people. But the direction is hard to argue with, and the size of the change is large.

It matters for more than mood. A 2010 meta-analysis led by Julianne Holt-Lunstad combined 148 studies following more than 300,000 people and found that those with stronger social relationships had about a 50 percent greater likelihood of survival over the study periods than those with weaker ones. **[Meta-analysis: Holt-Lunstad, Smith & Layton, 2010]** That is a correlation, not proof that friends make you live longer, and it isn't a reason to panic. It's a reason to stop treating friendship as a luxury.

## The rule that costs the most

Why would men, specifically, drift toward being fine and unknown?

The most useful single piece of research here is a 2017 meta-analysis by Joel Wong and colleagues. They pooled 78 samples, almost 19,500 people, to look at how conforming to traditional masculine norms relates to mental health. Overall, conformity had a modest unfavourable association with mental health and a moderate unfavourable association with seeking psychological help. **[Meta-analysis: Wong, Ho, Wang & Miller, 2017]**

But the more interesting result was in the detail. "Masculinity" is not one thing, and the norms didn't behave alike. Three were consistently linked with worse outcomes: self-reliance, power over women and the "playboy" norm. Others, such as putting work first, were not significantly related to any mental-health outcome.

Self-reliance is the one worth sitting with, because it doesn't look like a vice. It looks like competence. *Handle it yourself. Don't burden people. Figure it out.* Plenty of good men live by it, and it gets a lot done. It is also, according to this evidence, the rule most tied to men struggling quietly and not asking for help.

The psychologist Ronald Levant proposed a related idea in the early 1990s: that boys who are steered away from expressing vulnerable feelings can grow into men who find those feelings hard to put into words at all, a pattern he called "normative male alexithymia". **[Theory: Levant, 1992]** It's a theory rather than a settled finding, and it would be a mistake to diagnose anyone with it. But it describes something many men will recognise: not refusing to talk about feelings, but reaching for the words and finding the shelf empty.

The writer bell hooks made the cultural version of the argument in *The Will to Change* (2004): that the expectation for men to cut themselves off from their emotional lives harms men themselves, not only the people around them, and that love requires undoing it. **[Idea: bell hooks, The Will to Change, 2004; paraphrased]** You don't have to accept all of her framework to see the point. A rule that makes you less reachable is a rule that makes you less loved, however well it serves you at work.

## The arithmetic

Here's the part that gets left out of most writing on male loneliness: the problem is partly a matter of time.

In 2019 the communication researcher Jeffrey Hall published two studies on how friendships form. One surveyed adults who had recently moved and were looking for new friends; the other followed university students through their first weeks. Hall estimated that moving from acquaintance to casual friend took around 50 hours together, from casual friend to friend around 90, and from friend to close friend more than 200. Hours spent together socially counted for far more than hours spent working side by side. **[Study: Hall, 2019]**

Two hundred hours is roughly an evening a week for a year. Most adult men, between work, family and fatigue, don't spend that kind of time with anyone outside their household. Not because they don't value friends, but because nothing in adult life schedules it for them. School did. University did. Sports teams did. Then it stops, and nobody notices until the list of people who would know is short.

Aristotle got there first. In Book VIII of the *Nicomachean Ethics* he distinguishes friendships based on usefulness, on pleasure, and on goodness: the friend you value for who they are. The first two, he says, dissolve easily when the use or the pleasure ends. The third is rare and lasting, and it needs time and familiarity; he quotes a proverb that people can't really know each other until they have eaten a great deal of salt together. **[Philosophy: Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics VIII.3, paraphrased]**

Hall's two hundred hours is, more or less, the salt.

## What this does not mean

It doesn't mean men are broken, or that masculinity is the problem. The same research that links self-reliance to poorer mental health finds other traditionally masculine norms unrelated to it, and many men with strong friendships are traditionally masculine in every other way. It doesn't mean every man should be in therapy, although some would benefit and none should be ashamed to go. And it doesn't mean women and non-binary people don't face the same problem; loneliness is not a competition.

It means one specific, learnable thing: being fine on your own is a skill, and being reachable is a different skill, and most men were only taught the first. **[Moodius opinion]**

## Try this: The Reachability Audit (20 minutes)

1. Write down the names of the people who would *know*, without being told, if you weren't okay. Be strict.
2. Next to each name, estimate how many hours you spent with them, socially, in the last month. Phone calls count; group chats don't.
3. Pick one person who is on the edge of that list, someone who could be a close friend with time.
4. Put a recurring plan in your calendar with them: the same evening every two weeks, the same walk, the same game. Recurrence does what willpower can't.
5. The next time they ask how you are, give a true answer one sentence longer than you normally would.

## Three questions for your journal
1. If I were not okay, who would notice before I told them, and how would they notice?
2. Where did I learn that handling things alone is what a good man does, and what has that rule cost me?
3. Who in my life have I been "fine" with for years, and what would happen if I told them one true thing?

## Being known

Self-reliance gets men a long way. It builds careers, carries families through hard years and makes a person dependable. The evidence doesn't ask anyone to give it up. It asks for something smaller and harder: to let a few people close enough to know when you're not fine, and to spend the hours it takes for that to happen.

The loneliness of being fine is quiet, and that's what makes it dangerous. It doesn't announce itself. It just leaves the list of people who would know a little shorter each year, until one day you need it.
