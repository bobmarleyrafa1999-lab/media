Fifteen books across the traditions the page is widening into. Seven are public domain (free to read, and short quotes can go on posts with book and section). Eight are modern and in copyright: review them in Moodius words and quote at most one short credited line, never next to the shop.

**Rule:** a review goes out only after you have read the book. Each draft has `[MY TAKE]` gaps for your own reaction; fill them or cut them. Every draft ends on a question of one to three words. Check any page or chapter you mention against your own copy.

Format suggestion: a 5-slide carousel ("What I took from it" / one idea / one line / who it's for / question) or a 20-second reel with the cover blurred and abstract imagery (no author photos, per the no-faces rule).

### 1. Tao Te Ching, Laozi (tr. Legge)
Stoics tell you to hold firm. Laozi tells you to be like water. I read [MY TAKE: how long it took / when you read it] and kept coming back to chapter 8: "The highest excellence is like (that of) water." ### 2. The Analects, Confucius (tr. Legge)
Most self-help is about you alone in a room. Confucius thought you become good between people: in how you treat your parents, your friends, the stranger. One exchange stayed with me: a student says he doesn't want to do to others what he wouldn't want done to him, and Confucius answers, "you have not attained to that" (Book V). Not yet. [MY TAKE: where you fall short of your own rule.] It's a book of small duties done well. [MY TAKE]

### 4. Hayy ibn Yaqzan, Ibn Tufayl (tr. Ockley)
Written in 12th-century al-Andalus, eight centuries before anyone said "self-help". A boy grows up alone on an island, raised by a gazelle, and reasons his way from grief to the stars to something like wisdom. [MY TAKE: the part that moved you.] It's a novel, a philosophy lesson and a question at once: what would you work out about life if nobody had ever told you anything? [MY TAKE]
