History of medicine: starter tidbits

Every medical item is history, not advice, and the posts carry: “Historical interest only, not medical advice. Some traditional remedies are toxic.” No “ancient secret” framing. All are marked manual: read on 25 Oct with the exact sentence copied, and each one gets a second look before a deep dive.

HM001Ge Hong / Tu Youyou · China: Jin dynasty to Nobelmanual

A 4th-century Chinese first-aid handbook told readers to soak a handful of qinghao (sweet wormwood) in water and drink the juice for fevers. In the 1970s Tu Youyou read that line and it led her team to artemisinin, now a frontline malaria drug.

“A handful of qinghao immersed with 2 liters of water, wring out the juice and drink it all”

Tu Y. The discovery of artemisinin (qinghaosu) and gifts from Chinese medicine. Nature Medicine 17(10): 1217-1220, 2011 · quoting Ge Hong, Zhouhou Beiji Fang (Handbook of Prescriptions for Emergencies) · link

Caveat. Tu won the 2015 Nobel Prize for this. Modern artemisinin drugs are purified and dosed; drinking wormwood juice is NOT a malaria treatment. Historical interest only, not medical advice. Some traditional remedies are toxic.

HM002Tu Youyou · China: methodmanual

Her breakthrough came from reading the old recipe closely: it used cold water, not boiling. Heat was destroying the active compound, so her team switched to low-temperature extraction.

“extraction at a lower temperature might be necessary to preserve antimalarial activity”

Tu Y. Nature Medicine 17(10): 1217-1220, 2011 · Lasker Award essay · link

Caveat. Fragment of a longer sentence. Historical interest only, not medical advice. Some traditional remedies are toxic.

HM003Shennong Bencao Jing · China: Han dynastymanual

China's oldest surviving materia medica lists 365 medicines and sorts them into three grades by their effects and how toxic they are.

“This text records 365 medicinals and summarizes medicinal experiences up to the Han Dynasty.”

Zhao Z, Guo P, Brand E. A concise classification of bencao (materia medica). Chinese Medicine 13:18, 2018 · section on Shennong Bencao Jing · link

Caveat. The book is attributed to the mythical 'Divine Farmer' Shennong; it was compiled by unknown authors around the Han period. Grades = high/medium/low by effect and toxicity. Historical interest only, not medical advice. Some traditional remedies are toxic.

HM004Li Shizhen · China: Ming dynastymanual

Li Shizhen spent 27 years writing the Bencao Gangmu, a huge Ming-dynasty compendium of medicines; UNESCO lists it as world documentary heritage.

“Compiled and written by LI Shi-zhen (1518~1593), a medical expert of the Ming Dynasty (1368-1644) over a period of 27 years.”

UNESCO Silk Roads: Ben Cao Gang Mu (Compendium of Materia Medica) · web page · link

Caveat. First draft completed 1578; printed after his death (c. 1593-96). Entry counts (often given as 1,892) not checked yet. Historical interest only, not medical advice. Some traditional remedies are toxic.

HM005Hu Sihui · Mongol Yuan courtmanual

In 1330 Hu Sihui, dietician at the Mongol Yuan court, wrote the Yinshan Zhengyao, a food-as-medicine manual drawing on Chinese, Mongol and Arab sources.

“Hu Sihui incorporated a wide variety of dietary and medical knowledge from Chinese, Mongol, and Arab sources for his work.”

Medievalists.net (Sept 2024) summarising Niu H. et al., Yinshan Zhengyao: exploring the power of food and inheriting healthy thoughts. Food & Medicine Homology, 2024 · news summary of article · link

Caveat. Read the journal article itself before posting counts (232 foods, 174 medicinal plants come from the summary). English translation: Buell & Anderson, A Soup for the Qan. Historical interest only, not medical advice. Some traditional remedies are toxic.

HM006Rashid al-Din · Mongol Ilkhanatemanual

Under the Mongol Ilkhans, the vizier Rashid al-Din had a Chinese medical book translated into Persian, the Tanksuqname. Only the first of its four volumes survives, in Istanbul.

“The Tānksūqnāme was translated by order of Faḍl-Allah Rashīd al-Dīn Ibn ʿImād al-Dawla Abū al-Khayr (1247-1318).”

Acıduman A, Şems Ş, Arda B, Kırilen G. A Preliminary Study on Chapters Related to Human Anatomy in an Illustrated Persian Medical Book: Tānksūqnāme. Belleten 87(309): 415-437, 2023 · abstract · link

Caveat. Copy made in Tabriz, 10 December 1313; Ayasofya 3596, Süleymaniye Library. Genuine 'lost knowledge': three volumes are gone.

HM007Ibn al-Baytar · al-Andalus (Málaga)manual

Ibn al-Baytar, a botanist born in Málaga, compiled a dictionary of about 1,400 simple medicines from plants, animals and minerals.

“se enumeran alrededor de 1400 simples de origen vegetal, animal y mineral.”

Cabo-González AM. El Kitāb Mīzān al-Ṭabīb de Ibn al-Bayṭār. Folia Orientalia LVI, 2019 · introduction · link

Caveat. Evidence sentence is Spanish; translate on the post. Dates c. 1180/1187-1248. The big work is Kitāb al-Jāmiʿ. Historical interest only, not medical advice. Some traditional remedies are toxic.

HM008al-Zahrawi (Abulcasis) · al-Andalus (Córdoba)manual

Al-Zahrawi of Córdoba wrote a thirty-volume medical encyclopedia and drew the surgical instruments he used, probably the first illustrations of their kind.

“He included illustrations of the surgical instruments, probably the first of their kind”

Manaf F. Abulcasis, the pharmacist surgeon. Hektoen International 7(1), Winter 2015 · web article · link

Caveat. Hektoen is a medical-humanities journal, not peer-reviewed research; confirm '200 instruments' against a scholarly edition (Spink & Lewis 1973) before a deep dive.

HM009Ibn Zuhr (Avenzoar) · al-Andalus (Seville)manual

Ibn Zuhr of Seville tested a tracheotomy on a goat to show it was safe before anyone tried it on people.

“performed experimentally a tracheotomy on a goat, proving the safety of this operation in humans”

Karamanou M. et al. Avenzoar's (1091-1162) clinical description of cancer. JBUON 20(4): 1171-1174, 2015 · text · link

Caveat. Same paper says he gave the first detailed description of scabies and of a polypoid colorectal tumour; 'first' claims in medical history are often disputed, say 'one of the first'.

HM010Ibn al-Khatib · al-Andalus (Granada)manual

During the Black Death, Ibn al-Khatib of Granada argued from what he saw that plague spread by contagion, at a time when many denied it.

“determined that the bubonic plague was transmitted through contagion”

Lapham's Quarterly contributor page; scholarly study: Ober WB, Aloush N. The plague at Granada, 1348-1349: Ibn al-Khatib and ideas of contagion. Bull N Y Acad Med 58(4): 418-424, 1982 · web page; article not yet read · link

Caveat. Article text was blocked (captcha); read Ober & Aloush or Aberth, The Black Death (2005) pp. 114-116 for his own words before posting. He was later executed (1374), officially for heresy.

HM011Timbuktu manuscripts · West Africa (Mali)manual

An 18th-century Timbuktu manuscript gives diagnoses and cures using plants, animals and minerals, alongside prayers.

“The remedies described draw upon animal, plant, and mineral matter as well as prayers and Qur'anic verses.”

The Met, 'Curing Diseases and Effects both Apparent and Hidden', Mali, Timbuktu, 18th century · collection record 736515 · link

Caveat. HMML attributes the text Shifāʼ al-asqām to Aḥmad al-Raqqādī of the Kunta family (d. 1681 or 1684). Historical interest only, not medical advice. Some traditional remedies are toxic.

HM012Timbuktu manuscripts · West Africa (Mali)manual

Some Timbuktu medical handbooks name local trees in Bambara, and today's scholars cannot identify all of them. The knowledge is in the text, but the plant is lost.

“Most entries include the names of local trees and plants (in Bambara, as well as other local languages)”

Hill Museum & Manuscript Library, 'Medical Texts From Timbuktu: Local Pharmacological Remedies With Qur'anic Verses' · web article · link

Caveat. The article's example is a tree called Ṣukuru that the authors could not identify. Honest 'lost knowledge' angle, no 'ancient secret' framing.

HM013Timbuktu manuscripts · West Africa (Mali)manual

A 2011 survey counted more than 100,000 manuscripts in at least 408 private family libraries in Mali.

“A 2011 survey accounted for more than 100,000 such manuscripts preserved in no fewer than 408 private family libraries”

The Met, collection record 736515 · record text · link

Caveat. Counts vary by survey; quote the Met's figure with its date.

HM014Onesimus · West Africa to Bostonmanual

An enslaved West African man, Onesimus, told Cotton Mather how inoculation was done in Africa. In Boston's 1721 smallpox epidemic about 2% of inoculated people died versus about 14% of those who caught it naturally.

“had undergone an Operation, which had given him something of the Small-Pox, and would forever preserve him from it”

Cotton Mather, letter to the Royal Society, 1716, as quoted in Wikipedia 'Onesimus (Bostonian)'; numbers from Brown TH, JAMA 1988 and Boylston A, J R Soc Med 2012 ('The origins of inoculation') · Mather 1716 · link

Caveat. Death figures differ by source (242 or 280 inoculated; ~2% vs ~14%). Check Boylston 2012 before posting. Mather owned Onesimus: tell it as Onesimus's knowledge, not Mather's. Inoculation (variolation) was risky and is no longer used.

HM015Ebers Papyrus · Ancient Egyptmanual

The Ebers Papyrus, about 1550 BCE, is one of the oldest medical books known, with around 700 formulas and folk remedies.

“The scroll contains 700 magical formulas and folk remedies meant to cure afflictions”

Encyclopaedia Britannica, 'Ebers papyrus' · article · link

Caveat. Counts vary (700 to 800+ prescriptions) by edition. Held at Leipzig University Library. Historical interest only, not medical advice. Some traditional remedies are toxic.

HM016Ethiopian healers' books · Ethiopiamanual

Ethiopian healers kept handbooks that preserved and reused medical knowledge across generations; scholars study four such manuscripts in London and Paris.

“The analysis shows how the specialist knowledge was transmitted, preserved and reused.”

Burtea B. Traditional Medicine and Magic According to Some Ethiopian Manuscripts from European Collections. Aethiopica 18: 87-100, 2015 · abstract · link

Caveat. Abstract names no specific plants; read the article before posting remedies. Historical interest only, not medical advice. Some traditional remedies are toxic.

KH001Harbaugh, Mayr & Burghart · Neuroscience of giving2026-10-25

Brain scans show that even tax-like, compulsory gifts to charity light up reward areas, and voluntary giving lights them up more.

“even mandatory, tax-like transfers to a charity elicit neural activity in areas linked to reward processing”

Harbaugh WT, Mayr U, Burghart DR. Neural responses to taxation and voluntary giving reveal motives for charitable donations. Science 316: 1622, 2007 · abstract · link

Caveat. Small fMRI sample (19 students; DOI not yet resolved

KH002Aknin et al. · Psychology of givingmanual

People who had spent money on others in the past month reported more wellbeing in 120 of 136 countries surveyed.

“a positive relationship between personal well-being and spending on others in 120 of 136 countries”

Aknin LB et al. Prosocial spending and well-being: cross-cultural evidence for a psychological universal. J Pers Soc Psychol 104(4), 2013 (APA press release) · press release · link

Caveat. Correlational (Gallup World Poll 2006-08). A 2020 registered replication found smaller effects for the experimental version: say 'linked to', not 'causes'.

KH003Dunn, Aknin & Norton · Psychology of giving2026-10-25

A Science paper argued that how you spend money may matter as much for happiness as how much you earn; spending on others beat spending on yourself.

“we suggest that how people spend their money may be at least as important as how much money they earn”

Dunn EW, Aknin LB, Norton MI. Spending money on others promotes happiness. Science 319(5870): 1687-1688, 2008 · abstract · link

Caveat. Experiment was small (46 people; DOI not yet resolved

KH004Noetel et al. · Exercise and moodmanual

A 2024 BMJ review of randomised trials found walking or jogging, yoga and strength training all reduced depression, with walking/jogging among the strongest.

“walking or jogging (n=1210, κ=51, Hedges’ g −0.62, 95% credible interval −0.80 to −0.45)”

Noetel M et al. Effect of exercise for depression: systematic review and network meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials. BMJ 384: e075847, 2024 · abstract, results · link

Caveat. Not a replacement for treatment; say 'alongside', never 'instead of'. Many trials were low certainty.

PH001The Dhammapada · Buddhistmanual

The Dhammapada, Sacred Books of the East vol. X (Project Gutenberg #2017) · verse 5 · tr. F. Max Müller · link

A student said he did not want to do to others what he wouldn't want done to him. Confucius told him he hadn't got there yet.
