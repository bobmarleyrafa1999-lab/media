Words that hold

Which words and sentence shapes in your posts keep people watching, liking and following, what other creators' outlier hooks share, and the published research on why. Then a rewrite of every hook queued for 26 Oct to 1 Nov.

Built from 15 reels and 8 feed posts (Metricool, 6-23 Oct) and 168 on-topic creator hooks from the hook-intel library. Each number is a median of 2-10 posts, so these are leads to test, not findings. Metricool shows far fewer views than Instagram, so read the numbers post against post. The three newest reels, in the new look, all held fewer people, so part of that drop may be the look rather than the words.

The short version

  1. Open on another person or a scene the viewer is in. Hooks with a person or a conflict in them held 43% of viewers past 3 seconds. The rest held 29%.
  2. Ask, then make them wait for the second half. "Someone disrespected you today?" followed by "if it's true... if it's false..." was watched for 16.5 seconds. Every other reel got 2.5-5.7.
  3. Set up the obvious reading, then overturn it. "You don't have a discipline problem. You have a self-trust problem." is the only post that has brought follows (5).
  4. Put two things side by side that felt like one. "X vs Y" and "hurts more than" had the highest lift in the creator set, 5.8 times their usual views.
  5. Name the hurt, then end on relief. Pain words lift views across 17 creators, but sadness gets watched more than it gets shared.
  6. Stop opening with a number, a thinker's name, or "motivation". All three held fewer people.

Ranked patterns and why they work

1. The other-person scene, in the second person

Strongest on your page 43% vs 29% past 3 seconds

Information about the self is processed more deeply and remembered better than the same information about someone else: the self-reference effect, confirmed across 129 studies (Symons & Johnson 1997), and run by the brain's midline structures (Northoff et al. 2006). A threat from another person adds weight, because bad events get more attention than good ones of the same size (Baumeister et al. 2001). Question headlines that addressed the reader ("Is your boss...") got more clicks than statements (Lai & Farbrot 2014).

Frame 0 names a person and what they did to "you", in under 10 words. "Someone left you on read" beats "toxic people".

2. The question with a delayed second half

Longest watch 16.5 s vs 2.5-5.7 s, one reel

Curiosity rises when people notice a gap between what they know and what they want to know, most of all when the gap feels small enough to close soon (Loewenstein 1994). Waiting for an answer activates reward areas and improves memory for it (Kang et al. 2009), through the dopamine circuit and the hippocampus (Gruber et al. 2014). The waiting is rewarding in itself (Knutson et al. 2001).

Use a question only when the answer has two parts and the second lands after second 6. Always pay off both.

3. The overturned expectation

Only source of follows self-trust carousel, 5 follows

Dopamine neurons respond most to outcomes that differ from what was expected, the reward prediction error (Schultz et al. 1997). The eyes go to whatever changes expectations most (Itti & Baldi 2009). Line 1 sets the prediction ("you're lazy"), line 2 breaks it ("you're avoiding a feeling"). My reading, not a study: a follow is a bet that later posts will keep paying, and a reframe that changes how someone sees their own week is the best evidence of that.

Line 1 says what they already believe, in their words. Line 2 replaces it within 3 seconds.

4. X vs Y

Highest creator lift 5.8x, only 4 hooks

The same prediction error, plus a promise to settle a question the viewer already asks themself. Rest or avoidance? Grief or self-pity? The answer updates a belief about the self, so the self-reference effect applies too.

Both sides are feelings the viewer has had, never two kinds of people.

5. Pain words with a relief payoff

17 creators 1.81x, loneliness tops the set

Across 105,000 headline variants at Upworthy, each extra negative word raised click-through by about 2.3%, and sadness words had the clearest effect (Robertson et al. 2023). But sadness made New York Times articles less likely to be emailed, while awe made them more likely (Berger & Milkman 2012). Naming a feeling also calms it: labelling emotions reduced the amygdala's response (Lieberman et al. 2007).

Name the hurt in line 1, end on relief, awe or one small action. Your three newest reels got zero sends, and this is the most likely fix.

6. The correction

10 creators 1.70x; your "Not every insult deserves a reply" held 43%

"Stop" implies the viewer is losing something now, and losses weigh more than gains (Baumeister et al. 2001).

Correct a habit, never a person or a group.

7. "You" meaning anyone

Most likes "You can't make everyone like you", 11

People use generic "you" to turn a personal hurt into a general lesson (Orvell, Kross & Gelman 2017). It includes the viewer. Accusing "you" ("Motivation isn't coming... neither should you") corners them, and that reel reached 20.

Weak: a number, a name or a self-help noun first

Holds fewer 27%, 25% and 29%, against 33-37% without

A number says how long they'll be there and not why. A thinker's name is a reason to believe, not a reason to stay, so keep Marcus, Seneca and Epictetus on line 2. "Motivation" and "productivity" gave the two worst reels on the page.

Likes, follows and the social side

  • Likes beget likes. Teenagers liked photos more when they already had many likes, with more reward-area activity (Sherman et al. 2016); showing download counts made popular songs more popular (Salganik et al. 2006). The honest use is replying to comments in the first hour. Never buy likes or join engagement pods.
  • Talking about yourself feels rewarding. People gave up money to disclose things about themselves (Tamir & Mitchell 2012). That backs the 1-3 word question at the end of every post, answered first by Moodius.
  • Posts get sent when they're about the viewer and someone they know. Articles that went viral raised activity in brain areas for thinking about the self and about others (Scholz et al. 2017). "Send this to someone who..." works only once the reel has made them picture that person.
  • Moral words spread, mostly within a side. Each moral-emotional word raised retweets by about 20% (Brady et al. 2017). That's how outrage pages grow. Use those words about the viewer's own conduct, never against a group.

Retire as openers: numbers, "rules", "signs", "things", "motivation", "productivity", "discipline" unless it's reframed as a feeling, a thinker's name, "Did you know", "Save this".

Never: fake urgency, "wait for the end" when the end doesn't pay, rage bait about a group, "Comment YES if...", made-up numbers or studies, manipulation sold as a skill.
