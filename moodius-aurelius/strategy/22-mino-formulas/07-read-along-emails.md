### Day 1 · Two columns
Preview: The first line of a 1,900-year-old pocket book.

> Hello, and welcome to the read-along.
>
> Epictetus never wrote a book. A student, Arrian, took notes in his classes and later boiled them down into a small handbook, the Enchiridion. It opens like this:
>
> "Of things some are in our power, and others are not." (Enchiridion 1, tr. George Long)
>
> Today's task takes two minutes. Take one thing that's bothering you. Draw two columns: mine, not mine. Put every part of the worry in one or the other. Then cross out the second column.
>
> If you want, reply with one word: which column was longer? I read every reply.
>
> Moodius

### Day 2 · It's the story, not the thing
Preview: The most quoted line in the handbook, and why it's quoted.

> "Men are disturbed not by the things which happen, but by the opinions about the things" (Enchiridion 5, tr. George Long).
>
> Today, when something annoys you, write two sentences. First, what happened, in words a camera would agree with. Second, the story you added on top.
>
> Most of the heat is usually in the second sentence. That's the part that's yours to edit.
>
> Moodius

### Day 3 · Want it the way it goes
Preview: The handbook's answer to manifesting.

> In Moodius words: don't ask for things to happen the way you wish; wish for them to happen the way they do (after Enchiridion 8, tr. George Long).
>
> It sounds like giving up. It's closer to the opposite: it moves your effort from the weather to the umbrella.
>
> Today, pick one small thing you've been arguing with (the queue, the delay, the reply that hasn't come) and stop arguing with it for the whole day. Notice what you do with the energy instead.
>
> Moodius

### Day 4 · The leg and the will
Preview: He was lame. He said so. Then he said this.

> Epictetus was enslaved in Rome as a young man and walked with a limp; he called himself "a lame old man" (Discourses 1.16). In the handbook he puts it plainly:
>
> "Lameness is an impediment to the leg, but not to the will" (Enchiridion 9, tr. George Long).
>
> Today: name one real limit in your life, without dressing it up. Then name one thing it doesn't touch.
>
> Moodius

### Day 5 · Two words for difficult people
Preview: A Roman writer heard his rule second-hand. It's two words long.

> A Roman writer, Aulus Gellius, reports that Epictetus summed up his teaching in two words: bear and forbear (Attic Nights 17.19). Bear what other people do. Forbear the things you'd regret doing back.
>
> Today, practise once each. Bear one irritation without commentary. Forbear one reply you were about to send.
>
> Moodius

### Day 6 · The teacher who wanted you to leave
Preview: His lectures failed if you only clapped.

> Epictetus's own teacher, Musonius Rufus, used to try to put new students off, as a test of who would come back (Discourses 3.6). And if students only praised his lecture, Rufus said he had spoken for nothing (Discourses 3.23).
>
> Today, ask one person you trust for one honest criticism of something you made or did. Say thank you, and don't defend it.
>
> Moodius

### Day 7 · What he did with almost nothing
Preview: The last day, and the story we saved for it.

> An ancient commentator, Simplicius, records that Epictetus, who had little and never married, took in a baby that a friend was going to abandon, and brought it up himself. (We have this from a writer centuries later, via George Long's introduction to the Discourses, so treat it as testimony rather than record.)
>
> A man who taught that very little is in our power used the little he had for that.
>
> Last task: one kindness today that nobody will thank you for.
>
> That's the read-along. If it helped, the Moodius journal turns these seven days into thirty, with a page for each: [The Unexamined Self, £14](/r/journal?src=readalong_7). And if it didn't, reply and tell me why. Both are useful.
>
> Moodius
