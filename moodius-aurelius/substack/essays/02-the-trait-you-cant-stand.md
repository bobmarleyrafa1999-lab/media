# The Trait You Can't Stand in Other People

*Jung called it the shadow. Lab psychology has tested a narrower cousin of the idea. Neither is a cure, but together they turn irritation into a useful question.*

Most people have a type. Not a romantic type: an irritating one. The colleague who talks over everyone. The friend who is always the victim. The man at the gym who needs to be seen. You can feel your jaw tighten before they've finished a sentence.

Some of that is simply good judgement. Some behaviour is genuinely bad, and noticing it is not a symptom. But if you've ever been surprised by the *size* of your reaction, by how much of your evening a stranger's arrogance can occupy, there's an old idea worth taking seriously, and a newer body of evidence worth knowing about before you take it too far.

## What Jung meant

Carl Jung, the Swiss psychiatrist who founded analytical psychology, used the word *shadow* for the parts of our personality we don't accept as ours. Not only the dark parts: anything that doesn't fit the image we hold of ourselves. A man who prides himself on being easy-going may disown his own ambition. A woman who sees herself as generous may disown her resentment. Those traits don't disappear, Jung argued; they go out of view, and we tend to meet them again in other people, where they are easier to dislike. He called this projection. He developed the idea across his career, most fully in his 1951 work *Aion*. **[Interpretive framework: Jung, Aion, 1951; paraphrased]**

It's a powerful idea, and it has become a booming online genre. "Shadow work" now comes in thirty-day challenges, printable worksheets and prompts that promise to heal your inner wounds.

So it's worth being clear about what kind of claim this is. Jung's shadow is a concept from a clinical and interpretive tradition, built from case observation and from his reading of myth, religion and literature. It is not a finding from controlled research, and "shadow work" as sold online has not been established as a treatment through clinical trials. **[Moodius note]** That doesn't make it worthless. Plenty of useful ideas about human beings are interpretive. It does mean we should hold it as a lens, not a diagnosis, and be suspicious of anyone who says a worksheet will heal trauma.

## What the lab has tested

The more interesting story is that psychologists, working in a completely different tradition, have tested something close to one part of Jung's idea.

In 1997 Leonard Newman, Kimberley Duff and Roy Baumeister proposed a modern account of what Freud and Jung had called projection. Their argument ran roughly like this: when you try hard not to think about an unwanted trait in yourself, the effort keeps that trait mentally active, and an active idea colours how you read other people. You don't see your flaw in the mirror. You see it in the next ambiguous person who walks in. **[Study: Newman, Duff & Baumeister, 1997]**

The mechanism rests on an older and famous finding. In 1987 Daniel Wegner and colleagues asked people not to think about a white bear. Many found that the bear kept coming back, and some thought about it more afterwards than people who had been allowed to think about it freely. **[Study: Wegner et al., 1987]** Later research suggests the "rebound" is smaller and less consistent than the original experiment implied, but the basic point has held up: pushing a thought away is not the same as being rid of it.

In 2003 Jeff Schimel, Jeff Greenberg and Andy Martens tested whether projection might actually *protect* people. They gave participants false feedback suggesting they were high or low in repressed anger, then let some of them rate an ambiguous stranger. Those told they were high in anger rated the stranger as angrier. And those who were allowed to project showed the lowest accessibility of anger-related thoughts afterwards. A second study found the same pattern with dishonesty: people told they might be dishonest, and allowed to see dishonesty in someone else, went on to describe themselves as especially honest. **[Study: Schimel, Greenberg & Martens, 2003]**

These are small lab experiments with students, and they show a tendency, not a law. But the shape is recognisable. Seeing a feared trait in someone else can be a way of not seeing it in ourselves, and it seems to work, at least for a while.

## The Stoic version

Jung wasn't the first to suggest that our judgements of others might tell us something about ourselves. Marcus Aurelius, writing his private notebook in the second century, gave himself this instruction:

> "When thou art offended at any man's fault, forthwith turn to thyself…"
> *Meditations 10.30, tr. George Long*

He goes on, in the same passage, to suggest asking in what similar way you go wrong yourself: by treating money, pleasure or reputation as if they were the real goods. **[Philosophy: Meditations 10.30, paraphrased after the opening line]** Marcus wasn't describing an unconscious mechanism. He was proposing a discipline: use the moment of irritation as a cue to look inward. That is a more modest claim than Jung's, and arguably a more practical one.

## Why the way you look matters

Here the research on shame becomes important, because turning toward a trait you dislike can go two ways.

June Tangney and Ronda Dearing's work on shame and guilt draws a useful line. Guilt says *I did a bad thing*. It tends to point toward repair: an apology, a change. Shame says *I am bad*. It tends to point toward hiding, denial or anger at whoever made us feel it. **[Research synthesis: Tangney & Dearing, 2002]** If you discover your own arrogance and respond with shame, the most likely result is more of the very suppression that sent it into the shadow in the first place.

The alternative isn't letting yourself off. In a set of experiments, Juliana Breines and Serena Chen found that people who were encouraged to treat a recent failure with self-compassion were *more* motivated to improve, for example by studying longer for a retest, than people who were given a self-esteem boost. **[Study: Breines & Chen, 2012]** Kindness toward yourself, in this research, didn't mean lower standards. It meant being able to look at the problem without flinching.

Put together, the evidence and the philosophy suggest a sequence. Notice the disproportionate reaction. Ask, without drama, whether a version of this lives in you. If it does, treat it the way you'd treat a mistake rather than a verdict. Then decide what, if anything, to do. **[Moodius practice]**

## An example, worked through

Imagine a manager who can't stand a colleague who "always needs credit". Every time the colleague mentions their own contribution in a meeting, the manager feels contempt. *(Illustration, not a real case.)*

The honest question isn't "Is the colleague annoying?" They might be. It's "Why does this take up so much space in me?" Perhaps the manager was raised to see wanting recognition as vulgar, works hard for it anyway, and never asks. The colleague is doing openly what the manager does in secret, and is getting what the manager quietly wants. Seen that way, the contempt is doing a job: it keeps the manager's own wish out of view.

Nothing in that insight requires the manager to like the colleague. It might, though, lead them to ask for recognition directly, which would be a better use of the energy.

## Try this: The Irritation Inventory (20 minutes, once a week)

1. List three people or types of people who irritated you more than the situation deserved this week.
2. For each, write the trait in one word: *arrogant, needy, lazy, fake, loud*.
3. For each word, write one specific moment in the last year when you showed a version of it, however small. If you genuinely can't find one, move on; not every irritation is a mirror.
4. For any you did find, rewrite it in guilt language, not shame language: "I did X" rather than "I am X".
5. Choose one small thing to do differently, or one need you could meet directly instead of resenting it in someone else.

## Three questions for your journal
1. Whose behaviour took up more of my attention this week than it deserved, and what exactly did they do?
2. What do I pride myself on never being, and when was the last time I came close?
3. If the trait I dislike most in others were secretly protecting something in me, what might it be protecting?

## Holding the idea lightly

The shadow is a metaphor with a long history and a real intuition behind it: that we see in others what we can't bear to see in ourselves. Lab psychology has tested a narrow, measurable piece of that intuition and found something like it. Neither tradition says that every dislike is a projection, or that excavating your shadow will cure anything.

What they offer together is a better question to ask in the moment your jaw tightens. Not *what is wrong with them?* but *what is this doing for me?* Asked kindly and often enough, that question is how people slowly become less defended and more whole, which is roughly what Jung hoped for, and roughly what Marcus asked of himself.
