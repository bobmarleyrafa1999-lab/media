# The Raise That Didn't Arrive Inside

*Money does make most people a little happier. The research is clearer than the slogans on both sides, and it leaves the oldest question about wanting exactly where Seneca left it.*

You get the promotion. Or the funding round closes, or the offer you've been working towards for three years finally arrives in writing. There's a day, maybe two, of something like elation. You tell the people who matter. You buy something.

And then, sometime in the following month, you notice that the feeling you expected to *have* is not the feeling you have. You're pleased, in a dry, factual way. But the thing that was supposed to settle inside you hasn't settled. The finish line has moved, quietly, to somewhere further on.

Two slogans try to explain this. One says money can't buy happiness. The other says that's something only comfortable people say. Both are wrong in useful ways.

## Money does help, mostly

For years the most quoted finding on this question came from a 2010 paper by Daniel Kahneman and Angus Deaton. Using Gallup survey data from the US, they found that people's evaluation of their lives kept rising with income, but their day-to-day emotional wellbeing seemed to level off at around $75,000 a year. **[Study: Kahneman & Deaton, 2010]** It became one of the most repeated numbers in popular psychology.

In 2021 Matthew Killingsworth published data that seemed to contradict it. Sampling people's feelings in real time through a phone app, he found that wellbeing kept rising with income well beyond that level, with no plateau. **[Study: Killingsworth, 2021]**

Instead of trading rebuttals, the two researchers did something unusual. With the psychologist Barbara Mellers acting as arbiter, they re-analysed the data together. Their 2023 paper reached a resolution. For most people, happiness rises steadily with the *logarithm* of income, which means each doubling of income brings roughly the same step up. Among the happiest people, the rise even speeds up. The plateau is real, but only for the least happy group, whose remaining unhappiness seems to come from things money doesn't touch. **[Adversarial collaboration: Killingsworth, Kahneman & Mellers, 2023]** The authors mention heartbreak, bereavement and clinical depression as examples.

So the honest answer is: yes, money helps most people, and it keeps helping. But notice what "log income" implies. Going from €40,000 to €80,000 buys you about the same step as going from €80,000 to €160,000. The raise that felt enormous at the start of your career has to be twice as large to feel the same later on. The finish line moves because the maths moves it.

## You adapt to what you get

There's a second reason the raise doesn't arrive inside, and it's older.

In 1978 Philip Brickman, Dan Coates and Ronnie Janoff-Bulman interviewed people who had recently won large lottery prizes, along with a comparison group and a group of people who had been paralysed in accidents. The lottery winners were not significantly happier than the comparison group, and they reported taking less pleasure in ordinary everyday activities. **[Study: Brickman, Coates & Janoff-Bulman, 1978]** The samples were small, and later research shows that some changes in life circumstances do shift long-term wellbeing. But the core observation has proved durable: people adapt to gains faster than they expect to, and what was thrilling becomes the baseline.

That's not a flaw. A mind that stayed permanently thrilled by every improvement wouldn't keep striving. But it does mean that if you're relying on the next achievement to change how you feel, you're relying on a feeling designed to fade.

## What you want it for matters more than how much

The third piece of evidence is about motivation rather than money itself.

In the 1990s Tim Kasser and Richard Ryan studied what they called the "dark side of the American dream". They found that people who placed relatively high importance on extrinsic aspirations (wealth, fame, image) compared with intrinsic ones (personal growth, relationships, contributing to a community) tended to report lower wellbeing. **[Study: Kasser & Ryan, 1996]** The research is correlational, and it doesn't say ambition is bad. It suggests that *why* you want something matters. Money pursued as a way to buy time, security or freedom for people you love tends to sit differently from money pursued as a scoreboard.

That distinction maps neatly onto the difference between the raise you can feel and the raise you can't. If the promotion was a means to something, you can feel the something. If it was a verdict on your worth, the verdict expires, and you need another.

## What Seneca knew

The Stoics lived in a society at least as status-obsessed as ours, and Seneca was one of the richest men in Rome, which makes him an awkward moralist on this subject. He knew it, and wrote about it often. But in the second of his letters to his friend Lucilius he gives the line that has outlasted everything else he said about money:

> "It is not the man who has too little, but the man who craves more, that is poor."
> *Seneca, Moral Letters 2.6, tr. Richard M. Gummere*

The claim isn't that wealth is bad or that poverty is noble. It's that poverty is partly a relationship between what you have and what you want. Raise the second faster than the first, and a rich person can feel poor indefinitely. **[Philosophy]**

Epicurus, a rival school, made a similar point from the other direction. In his *Letter to Menoeceus* he argues that a pleasant life depends far less on luxury than people suppose, and that the freedom of wanting little is itself one of the greatest goods. **[Philosophy: Epicurus, Letter to Menoeceus, paraphrased]**

Put the old and new together and a picture emerges. Money helps, on a log scale. You adapt to whatever you reach. And the craving that drives the next step can be pursued for reasons that leave you fuller or emptier. The only part of that you control directly is the last one. **[Moodius reading]**

## What this means for the next goal

None of this argues for giving up ambition. It argues for being precise about it.

**Name your "enough" in numbers.** Not a vague hope of comfort: an actual figure for the income, savings or security that would let you stop worrying about money. People who never define it find that it rises with every raise.

**Ask what the next goal is for.** If you can't name what it will buy you besides the feeling of having it, that feeling is the thing that will fade.

**Spend some of every gain on time.** A 2017 study by Ashley Whillans and colleagues found that people who spent money to save time, on tasks they disliked, reported greater life satisfaction. **[Study: Whillans et al., 2017]** It's one of the few uses of money that seems not to vanish into the baseline.

## Try this: The Enough Ledger (20 minutes)

1. Write down your last three significant wins: raises, deals, milestones. For each, write how long the good feeling lasted, honestly.
2. For each, write what it actually changed in your daily life a year later.
3. Write your "enough" figure: the amount that would remove financial fear. Be specific.
4. Write what the next goal is *for*, in one sentence that doesn't mention the goal itself.
5. Decide one way you will spend part of the next gain on time rather than things.

## Three questions for your journal
1. Which achievement did I expect to change me most, and what did it actually change?
2. If my income doubled tomorrow, what would I still be worried about?
3. Whose approval is the next goal really for, and would they notice if I stopped?

## After the goal

The raise didn't arrive inside because it was never going to. Not because money doesn't matter, but because the part of you that was waiting for it was waiting for something no number can deliver: the sense that you have finally done enough to be allowed to rest.

That permission doesn't come from the next promotion. It comes, if it comes at all, from deciding what enough means and noticing when you've reached it. Seneca put it in nine words two thousand years ago. The research has spent fifty years arriving at roughly the same place, with better numbers.
