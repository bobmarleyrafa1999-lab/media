# Writing to No One

*Marcus Aurelius kept a notebook he never meant anyone to read. The research on why the habit helps is smaller, stranger and more useful than the journaling industry admits.*

The most famous journal in Western philosophy was not written for readers. Marcus Aurelius, emperor of Rome, spent the last decade of his life filling a notebook with reminders to himself, much of it, historians think, while he was on campaign. The book has come down to us under a Greek title usually translated as *To Himself*. It repeats itself. It scolds. It returns to the same handful of ideas the way you return to a sore tooth. It reads less like a treatise than like a man trying to stay the person he meant to be.

Two thousand years later, journaling has become a product. There are guided journals for gratitude, manifestation, shadow work and "healing", apps that turn your entries into mood graphs, and a steady supply of posts promising that ten minutes with a pen will change your life.

The honest version is more interesting. Writing about what you feel does help, on average, a little. It helps more when you do it in a particular way. And for some people, some of the time, it makes things worse. All three of those are worth knowing before you buy another notebook.

## What the research actually found

The modern study of this habit starts in 1986. The psychologist James Pennebaker and his student Sandra Beall asked university students to write for fifteen minutes on four consecutive days. Some wrote about superficial topics. Others wrote about the most upsetting experiences of their lives, and one group was asked to write about both the facts and their deepest feelings. In the months that followed, the students who wrote about facts and feelings together visited the campus health centre less often than the control group, even though the writing itself left them more upset in the short term. **[Study: Pennebaker & Beall, 1986]**

That finding launched hundreds of follow-up experiments. In 2006 Joanne Frattaroli pooled 146 randomised studies of "experimental disclosure", mostly writing tasks, into a single meta-analysis. The average effect was positive and statistically reliable, and it was small: a correlation of about r = .075. **[Meta-analysis: Frattaroli, 2006]** In plain terms, across many people, writing about difficult experiences does some good. In any one person, you might struggle to notice it.

That is a less exciting headline than "journaling rewires your brain", but it is the truthful one, and it has a useful implication. If the average effect is small, then *how* you write probably matters more than *whether* you write.

## Why putting it into words might help

One clue comes from a different kind of experiment. In 2007 Matthew Lieberman and colleagues showed people emotionally charged photographs while scanning their brains. When participants chose a word to label the emotion in a face ("angry", "scared") rather than, say, matching it to another face, activity in the amygdala, a region involved in detecting threat, went down, and activity in a region of the right prefrontal cortex went up. **[Study: Lieberman et al., 2007]**

It's tempting to turn this into "naming a feeling tames it". The study doesn't show that. It shows a short-term change in brain activity during a lab task with pictures of strangers. What it does suggest is that turning a raw state into language is not a passive act. Choosing words is a small piece of work, and that work seems to change the state you're describing, at least a little.

The Stoics would not have been surprised. Their whole method rested on the idea that we are disturbed less by events than by the judgements we make about them, and that judgements can be examined once they are put into words. **[Philosophy]**

## When writing makes it worse

Here is the part the guided journals tend to leave out.

There is a well-studied habit called rumination: repetitive, passive focus on your distress, its causes and its consequences, without moving toward any solution. Reviews of the research link it to longer low moods and worse problem solving. **[Review: Nolen-Hoeksema, Wisco & Lyubomirsky, 2008]** Rumination doesn't need a pen, but a pen will happily carry it. A journal can become a place where you write the same grievance every night in slightly different words, and each time you rehearse it, it gets a little more fluent.

Picture someone who has been passed over for a promotion and starts journaling about it. Night one, they write what happened. Night two, what it means about their manager. Night three, what it means about them. By night ten the notebook is a closing argument they have rehearsed until it sounds like the truth. That's not a failure of willpower. It's what a mind does when it is asked to circle the same wound with no change of angle. *(Illustration, not a real case.)*

The research suggests a way out, and it's the same move Marcus made without knowing he was making it.

## The change of angle

The psychologists Ethan Kross and Özlem Ayduk have spent years studying what they call self-distancing. When people reflect on a painful experience from an immersed perspective, re-living it through their own eyes, they tend to get pulled back into the feeling. When they reflect from a distanced perspective, as a fly on the wall, or by using their own name or "you" instead of "I", they tend to report less emotional reactivity and less rumination, and to make more sense of what happened. **[Review: Kross & Ayduk, 2017]** The effects vary from person to person and much of the work comes from the lab, but the pattern has held across many studies.

Now go back to Marcus. He rarely writes "I". He writes "thou". He talks to himself as a teacher would talk to a promising student who keeps making the same mistake. In Book 4 he describes the notebook's purpose almost exactly:

> "For nowhere either with more quiet or more freedom from trouble does a man retire than into his own soul… Constantly then give to thyself this retreat, and renew thyself; and let thy principles be brief and fundamental."
> *Meditations 4.3, tr. George Long*

It would be too neat to say Marcus discovered self-distancing. He was writing inside a philosophical tradition that taught people to examine their impressions from the outside. **[Moodius reading]** But the overlap is striking: the oldest surviving journal of its kind is written in the second person, and the best modern evidence on reflective writing suggests that the second person is where some of the benefit lives.

Seneca, a century earlier, described a different discipline: the evening review. In his essay *On Anger* he says that each night, once the lamp was out, he went back over his entire day, hiding nothing from himself and sparing himself nothing, and that he slept better for it. **[Philosophy: Seneca, On Anger 3.36, paraphrased]** What's notable is the tone. He doesn't put himself on trial. He reviews, notes where he could have done better, and lets it go until tomorrow.

## What this means on a Tuesday evening

Put the research and the philosophy side by side and a practice falls out of it. None of what follows has been tested as a package; it's a way of applying findings that have been tested separately. **[Moodius practice]**

1. **Give it a container.** The original studies used fifteen to twenty minutes. Set a timer. A container stops reflection becoming a loop.
2. **Write the facts, then the feeling, then the meaning.** Pennebaker's strongest condition asked for facts *and* feelings together. Meaning comes last, once you've seen both.
3. **When you notice the same sentence twice, change the angle.** Switch from "I" to "you" or to your own name. Write the next paragraph as a calm friend describing what happened to someone they respect.
4. **End with something small you will do.** Not a resolution. One action, one conversation, one thing you'll stop doing tomorrow.
5. **Don't publish it.** Not even to yourself, in a sense: you are not writing the definitive account. You are writing to no one, which is what makes it possible to be honest.

## Try this: The Distanced Page (15 minutes)

1. Write for five minutes about something that has been bothering you, in the first person, as it comes.
2. Read it back and underline any sentence you've written before, here or in your head.
3. For the next five minutes, rewrite the situation in the second person: "You were passed over. You wanted…" Keep going until the tone sounds like someone on your side.
4. For the last five minutes, write the evening review in Seneca's spirit: what you did well today, where you could do better, and one thing you'll try tomorrow.
5. Close the notebook. Don't reread it tonight.

## Three questions for your journal
1. What have I written about more than three times this month, and what would I say about it if it had happened to a friend?
2. Which feeling did I find hardest to name today, and what is the most exact word for it?
3. If my notebook were the only place I could be completely honest, what would I admit in it this week that I haven't?

## The point of writing to no one

Marcus's notebook survived by accident. He didn't write it to be read, and that's exactly why it still reads as true. The research doesn't promise that a journal will fix your life. It suggests something more modest and more useful: that putting your inner life into words, from a slight distance, for a set time, can loosen what you're carrying a little, and that a little, repeated every evening, is how most things that matter change.

You don't need a guided journal for that. You need a page, fifteen minutes and the willingness to talk to yourself the way a good teacher would.
