## Brief (not for publication)
**Thesis.** Being needed delivers a dependable version of what we want from love: closeness, purpose, proof that we matter. For people who doubt they'd be loved simply for who they are, usefulness can become the way they earn a place. Psychologists call one version of this *unmitigated communion*: caring for others to the exclusion of the self. It isn't the giving that's the problem. It's the self-neglect and the difficulty receiving that come with it, which quietly keep others from loving the giver back.

**Counterarguments, and how the essay handles them**
1. *Being needed is genuinely good for you.* Yes: a prospective study linked giving support to lower mortality. The essay leads with that, and locates the problem in self-neglect, not generosity.
2. *In many cultures, family duty is love, not a disorder.* Agreed. The essay doesn't pathologise care or obligation. It describes a pattern that hurts the carer, measured by distress and an inability to accept help.
3. *Calling it a "pattern" turns kind people into patients.* The essay avoids diagnosis and frames it as a question each reader can ask.

# Why Being Needed Can Feel Like Being Loved

*Giving is one of the best things a person can do, and there's evidence for it. But there's a way of giving that keeps love at arm's length, and it's common among the people everyone relies on.*

Every family, friend group and office has one. The person who remembers birthdays, drives people to the airport, picks up at two in the morning, knows how to fix it. They're rarely the loudest person in the room, but if they left, the room would stop working.

Ask them how they are and they'll say fine, and turn the question back to you. Offer to help them and they'll find a reason you shouldn't. They are deeply loved, and often, if they're honest, they don't quite feel it.

This essay is about why that happens, and about the difference between being needed and being loved, which can feel identical from the inside for years.

## First, giving is good

It would be easy, and wrong, to turn this into an argument against generosity. The evidence points the other way.

In a study published in 2003, Stephanie Brown and colleagues followed older married couples in the US for five years. People who provided practical help to friends, relatives and neighbours, or emotional support to their spouse, were significantly less likely to die during the study. Receiving support, once giving was taken into account, made no difference. The results held after controlling for health, personality and other factors. **[Prospective study: Brown, Nesse, Vinokur & Smith, 2003]** It's an observational study, so it can't prove that helping causes longer life, but it's consistent with a lot of other work suggesting that giving is part of what makes a life feel meaningful.

So being needed isn't a trap in itself. It's often the best of us. The problem is narrower and harder to see.

## When caring turns into self-erasure

In the late 1990s the health psychologist Vicki Helgeson and her colleague Heidi Fritz described a pattern they called *unmitigated communion*: focusing on others to the exclusion of oneself. **[Theory: Helgeson & Fritz, 1998]** It isn't the same as being caring. In their research, ordinary communion, caring about others and relationships, was linked to good outcomes. Unmitigated communion was different. It was associated with neglecting one's own needs, getting over-involved in other people's problems, and finding it hard to accept help, and with more distress and depressive symptoms. **[Studies: Fritz & Helgeson, 1998]**

The difference isn't how much you give. It's whether there's still a *you* in the equation: whether your own needs count, and whether you can let anyone meet them.

## Why usefulness feels like love

Being needed offers something that love, frustratingly, doesn't: certainty.

If people need you, you know where you stand. Your place is guaranteed by what you do. Every request is a small confirmation that you matter. Love, by contrast, asks you to believe you'd be wanted even if you stopped being useful, and that's unprovable in the moment. You can't earn it, measure it or secure it in advance.

For someone who learned early that attention came when they were helpful, good, easy or competent, the trade is understandable. Usefulness becomes a way to deserve a place at the table. And because the place is deserved, it never feels quite safe. You have to keep earning it. **[Moodius reading]**

Seneca put his finger on the logic of this in a letter about friendship, though he was describing the opposite side of the bargain:

> "He who begins to be your friend because it pays will also cease because it pays."
> *Seneca, Moral Letters 9, tr. Richard M. Gummere*

Seneca meant this as a warning against people who befriend you for advantage. But it applies, uncomfortably, to the person who makes themselves useful in order to be kept. If your place in someone's life rests on what you provide, part of you will always expect it to end when the providing does.

## The receiving problem

The hardest part of this pattern is that it blocks love from arriving even when it's offered.

The people around the reliable friend often want to give back. They notice the tiredness. They offer to help. And they're turned down, kindly and firmly, again and again. Eventually they stop offering. The reliable friend then experiences exactly what they feared: that nobody looks after them. The loneliness is real. It's also partly self-made.

The social psychologists Jennifer Crocker and Amy Canevello studied something related in first-year university roommates. They distinguished between *compassionate goals*, wanting to support someone for their sake, and *self-image goals*, wanting to be seen in a particular way, including as helpful or good. In their work, compassionate goals tended to create mutual support over time: giving made people more likely to receive, and relationships grew closer. Self-image goals tended to undermine it. **[Studies: Crocker & Canevello, 2008]** The behaviour can look identical. What differs is whether the giving is about the other person or about securing one's own standing.

Erich Fromm made a similar distinction half a century earlier in *The Art of Loving*: mature love gives out of fullness, not as a way to fill an emptiness or to be paid back. **[Idea: Fromm, The Art of Loving, 1956; paraphrased]**

## How to tell the difference

None of this can be diagnosed from the outside, and nobody should be told their kindness is a symptom. But a few questions tend to separate the giving that connects from the giving that protects:
- Can you say no to a request without guilt that lasts the rest of the day?
- When did you last ask someone for help, and how did it feel?
- If you stopped being useful for a month, do you believe your closest people would still want you around?
- Do you know what you need, apart from what others need from you?

If those questions are uncomfortable, that isn't a verdict. It's a direction.

## Try this: The Receiving Practice (one week)

1. Each day this week, accept one offer of help you'd normally turn down, however small: a coffee, a lift, someone else doing the washing-up.
2. Don't repay it the same day. Notice the urge to.
3. Once this week, ask for something without explaining why you need it.
4. Each evening, write one sentence: *What did it feel like to be given to?*
5. At the end of the week, tell one person something you're struggling with that they can't fix. Just let them know.

## Three questions for your journal
1. Who would I be to the people I love if I couldn't do anything for them for a month?
2. When did I learn that being helpful was how I earned my place, and is it still true?
3. What would I let someone do for me if I believed I deserved it without paying it back?

## Being loved for nothing

Being needed is one of the great goods of a human life. The research says so, and so does anyone who has sat with a friend through a bad night. But it is a different thing from being loved, and the difference matters most to the people who give the most.

Love can't be earned the way a place on the team is earned. It has to be received, which means letting people close enough to see you when you're not useful, and trusting that they'll stay. That is harder than any favour you'll ever do. It's also the only way the people who rely on you will ever get to give something back.
