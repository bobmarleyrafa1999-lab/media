## Brief (not for publication)
**Thesis.** Everyone inherits ways of being, not just traits: how a household handled conflict, affection, money, illness, failure. Most of it arrives before we can examine it, so it feels like personality rather than a habit. The best-studied versions (maltreatment and attachment) show transmission is real but far from fixed, and the people who break a pattern tend to share a few things: someone who supported them, a coherent account of what happened, and a decision.

**Counterarguments, and how the essay handles them**
1. *Blaming parents is a cop-out.* The essay doesn't blame. It describes, and puts the responsibility on what the reader does next.
2. *A lot of this is genetic, not learned.* Granted: the essay says transmission runs through several routes and that the attachment research could only explain part of it.
3. *Abuse research doesn't apply to ordinary families.* Agreed that it's the extreme case. The essay uses it because it's where transmission has been measured most carefully, and says so.

# The Things We Inherit Without Realising

*Families pass down more than eyes and surnames. They pass down ways of handling anger, money, silence and love. The research says the inheritance is real, and that it is not a sentence.*

At some point most adults hear their father's voice come out of their own mouth. It might be a phrase, a tone, a sigh at the right moment. It might be bigger: the way you go silent when someone's upset with you, the way money makes you anxious even when there's enough, the way illness is something you hide.

None of these feel inherited. They feel like *you*. That's the point. The things we inherit most completely are the ones we never had a chance to look at, because we learned them before we had words.

## An old proverb

The prophet Ezekiel quotes a saying that was apparently common in his time:

> "The fathers have eaten sour grapes, and the children's teeth are set on edge."
> *Ezekiel 18:2, King James Version*

He quotes it in order to reject it. The rest of the chapter argues that each person answers for their own life, not their parents'. That tension, between the weight of what's handed down and the claim that we're not bound by it, is the subject of this essay. It's also, it turns out, roughly where the research lands. **[Philosophy: Ezekiel 18, read as literature]**

## What gets passed down

Inheritance in this sense isn't mysterious. Children learn what's normal from what they see daily: how adults repair after a fight or don't, whether feelings are named or managed, whether money is talked about or feared, whether affection is spoken or shown through doing. Family therapists in the tradition of Murray Bowen describe families as emotional systems in which patterns move from one generation to the next unless someone notices them. **[Theory: Bowen family systems theory, an interpretive framework]** Jung went further and suggested that what parents leave unlived and unexamined often shapes children most of all. **[Idea: Jung, interpretive]**

Those are frameworks, not findings. To see how strong the pull of a family pattern actually is, you have to look where it's been measured.

## Where it's been measured

The most carefully studied inheritance is also the darkest: whether children who are maltreated go on to maltreat their own children. In 1987 Joan Kaufman and Edward Zigler reviewed the evidence and estimated that roughly one in three people who had been abused or severely neglected went on to maltreat their own children. That is about six times the general rate, so the risk is real. But it also means most did not. The authors concluded that "unqualified acceptance of the intergenerational hypothesis is unwarranted." **[Review: Kaufman & Zigler, 1987]**

A gentler version comes from attachment research. In a 1995 meta-analysis of 18 samples and 854 parent–child pairs, Marinus van IJzendoorn found that a parent's way of talking about their own childhood, coded in a structured interview, matched their infant's attachment pattern (secure or insecure) about 75 percent of the time. That's a strong link. But when he tried to explain *how* it passes on, the obvious route, how sensitive and responsive the parent was, accounted for only part of it. He called the unexplained remainder the "transmission gap." **[Meta-analysis: van IJzendoorn, 1995]**

Read together, these findings say two things at once. Families do pass things down, sometimes strongly. And the passing-down isn't a simple copy. Something in between can change it.

## Who breaks the pattern

A review by the Australian Institute of Family Studies gathered what's known about the people who don't repeat the cycle of maltreatment. Several factors keep appearing: emotional support from at least one adult in childhood; a supportive partner later; fewer stressful life events; being able to give a coherent, honest account of what happened, often with anger about it rather than excuses for it; and a conscious resolve not to repeat it. **[Review: Australian Institute of Family Studies, summarising several studies]**

That list is worth reading slowly, because almost none of it is about willpower alone. It's about relationships, and about making sense of the story.

Attachment researchers have a term for people who grew up with difficult care but end up secure as adults: "earned security." The evidence on how to identify them is messier than the phrase suggests. In a long-running study in Minnesota, Glenn Roisman and colleagues found that people who *described* hard childhoods in a coherent way didn't always have the harsh early care they reported, and some had struggled with low mood. But those who had been insecure as infants and were secure at 19 did as well in adult relationships as people who'd been secure all along. **[Study: Roisman, Padrón, Sroufe & Egeland, 2002]** The route isn't fully understood. The destination is real.

## The ordinary version

Most readers didn't grow up with maltreatment. The inheritance is quieter: a father who never apologised, so apologising feels like losing; a mother who coped by staying busy, so rest feels like danger; a house where nobody raised their voice, so any conflict feels like a crisis. These aren't scars. They're defaults, and defaults are hard to see precisely because they've always been there.

It's worth being honest about one complication. Not everything that runs in families is learned. Temperament is partly heritable, so a quick-tempered parent and a quick-tempered child may share genes as well as habits, and the studies above can't fully separate the two. That's part of why van IJzendoorn's gap exists. But it doesn't change the practical question much. Whether a reaction came to you through your genes, your kitchen table or both, the moment you notice it is the same moment, and what you do next is still yours. **[Moodius note]**

The research on the extreme cases suggests what helps with the ordinary ones. You can't change a pattern you can't describe. Describing it isn't blaming anyone. It's taking the inheritance out of the walls and putting it on the table, where you can decide what to keep. **[Moodius reading]**

Some of what you inherited is worth keeping. A grandfather's patience, a mother's way with strangers, a family's stubborn loyalty. The point isn't to reject your family. It's to stop receiving the whole estate without reading the will.

## Try this: The Inheritance Inventory (20 minutes)

1. Write five headings: **Anger. Money. Affection. Conflict. Failure.**
2. Under each, write one sentence about what your household did with it. Not what was said. What was done.
3. Under that, write what you do with it now.
4. Mark each one **Keep**, **Return** or **Not sure**.
5. Pick one **Return**. Write one sentence you could say, or one thing you could do, the next time it shows up. Tell one person you trust that you're trying it.

## Three questions for your journal
1. Which phrase, tone or reaction of a parent have I caught in myself, and when does it appear?
2. What was never talked about in my house, and what do I still not talk about?
3. What did my family do well that I want to make sure I pass on?

## Reading the will

Ezekiel's proverb survived because it describes something real: children do feel the effects of what their parents did. His answer survived because it's also real: we are not bound to repeat it. The research sits between them. The pull of a family is strong, measurable and often invisible. It is also, for most people, breakable, especially with someone beside them and a clear account of where they came from.

You didn't choose what was handed to you. You do get to choose what you hand on.
