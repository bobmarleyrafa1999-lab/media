There are two people at the gym at six in the morning. They have the same programme, the same app, the same alarm. One is there because he wants to be strong for the next forty years. The other is there because he can’t stand what he sees in the mirror.

From outside, you can’t tell them apart. From inside, they’re living different lives. One is building something. The other is serving a sentence. And it’s the second one, sooner or later, who quits.

A generation of improvers

It’s hard to overstate how much of modern life is organised around becoming better. Routines, habit trackers, productivity systems, morning protocols, books promising a new you. Much of it is useful. But something has shifted underneath it.

In 2019 Thomas Curran and Andrew Hill pooled 164 samples of more than 41,000 college students in the US, Canada and the UK, measured between 1989 and 2016. Every kind of perfectionism they measured rose. The sharpest rise, about 33 percent, was in socially prescribed perfectionism: the belief that other people expect you to be perfect and will judge you if you aren’t. [Meta-analysis: Curran & Hill, 2019]

That’s the soil self-rejection grows in. If you believe you are only acceptable when you’re improved, improvement stops being a choice. It becomes the price of being allowed to exist.

Does being hard on yourself work?

Most people who are harsh with themselves believe it’s what keeps them going. Take away the inner critic, they think, and they’d stop trying.

The evidence doesn’t support them. Theodore Powers, Richard Koestner, David Zuroff and colleagues followed people pursuing real goals (weight loss, music practice, studying) over periods from a week to six months. Across their studies, people higher in self-criticism made less progress on their goals, including, in one study, less actual weight loss over six months as measured on a scale. [Studies: Powers et al., 2011] These are correlations, so they can’t prove that self-criticism causes slower progress. But they’re the opposite of what the inner critic promises.

What self-compassion actually does

Self-compassion has a soft reputation, as if it means bubble baths and lowered expectations. The psychologist Kristin Neff, who developed the best-known measure of it, defines it more sharply: treating yourself with the kindness you’d offer a friend, recognising that failure is part of being human rather than proof you’re uniquely flawed, and holding painful feelings in awareness without being swallowed by them. [Theory: Neff, 2003]

Juliana Breines and Serena Chen tested whether that makes people lazy. In four experiments, they had people think about a weakness, a moral mistake or a failed test, then nudged some of them toward self-compassion, for example by writing to themselves about the weakness from a kind and understanding perspective for three minutes. Compared with control groups, the self-compassion groups were more motivated to improve: more willing to work on the weakness, more inclined to make amends for the mistake, and more willing to study after failing. [Experiment: Breines & Chen, 2012]

The logic is simple once you see it. If failure means you’re worthless, every attempt is a threat, and avoiding it is self-protection. If failure means you’re human, you can look at it, learn from it and try again.

An emperor’s instruction to himself

Marcus Aurelius had every reason to be harsh with himself: an empire to run, a philosophy that asked a great deal, a notebook full of reminders about how far he fell short. Yet one of those reminders reads:

“Be not disgusted, nor discouraged, nor dissatisfied, if thou dost not succeed in doing everything according to right principles; but when thou hast failed, return back again.” Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 5.9, tr. George Long

He goes on to say that philosophy shouldn’t be approached like a harsh schoolmaster, but used gently, the way someone with sore eyes uses a soothing remedy. [Philosophy: Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 5.9, rest paraphrased] The Stoics are often recruited as patron saints of grinding discipline. On this point, at least, Marcus was gentler with himself than most of the people who quote him.

Standards are not the problem

It would be easy to read all this as a case for lowering the bar. It isn’t. The trouble isn’t wanting a lot from yourself. It’s what happens when you fall short.

A person with high standards and a kind relationship to failure looks at a bad week and asks what went wrong. A person with high standards and a contemptuous relationship to failure looks at the same week and asks what’s wrong with them. The first question has answers you can act on. The second mostly has answers that make you want to hide. [Moodius reading]

There’s also a difference between rejecting a behaviour and rejecting a self. Some things you do probably should change: the drinking, the temper, the way you leave things half-finished. Wanting them gone is healthy. The shift happens when “I did a thing I don’t want to keep doing” turns into “I am the kind of person who can’t be trusted.” One is a project. The other is a sentence.

How to tell which one you’re doing

The difference between improvement and rejection isn’t in the plan. It’s in the story underneath it. A few signs that the project has turned into a verdict: - A missed day feels like evidence about who you are, not just a missed day. - You can’t say what you’re improving towards, only what you’re getting away from. - Progress brings relief rather than satisfaction, and the relief is short. - You’d be embarrassed if a friend talked to themselves the way you talk to you.

None of these means you should stop trying. They mean the fuel is wrong. [Moodius reading]

Try this: The Friend’s Letter (15 minutes)

  1. Write down the thing about yourself you’re most trying to change.
  2. Write two lines about it in the voice you usually use with yourself. Notice the tone.
  3. Now imagine a friend you respect told you they were struggling with exactly this. Write them a short letter: honest about the problem, kind about the person, practical about one next step.
  4. Read the letter back as if it were written to you.
  5. Keep the next step from the letter. Drop the two lines from step 2.

Three questions for your journal

  1. What am I trying to improve because I want to, and what because I can’t stand myself as I am?
  2. Whose voice is my inner critic, and would I take advice from that person today?
  3. What would I do differently tomorrow if failing at it didn’t say anything about my worth?

Building something

Self-improvement at its best is an act of care: for your future self, for the people who rely on you, for the life you want to have built. At its worst, it’s a sentence served one routine at a time, by someone who doesn’t believe they’re allowed to stop.

The gym is the same, the app is the same, the alarm is the same. The question is which of the two men is getting up. The research, and an emperor’s notebook, both suggest that the one who can forgive a missed morning is the one still there in a year.

If self-criticism has become constant, or tips into self-harm, disordered eating or thoughts that you’d be better off gone, please talk to a GP or call Samaritans on 116 123 (UK, any hour).
