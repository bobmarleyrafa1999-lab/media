Most people have one. The relationship that felt like more than all the others put together. The texts you waited for with your whole body. The fights that ended in reunions so intense they almost justified the fights. When it ended, nothing afterwards felt as real.

And many people also have the other one. The person who was kind, consistent, interested, there. Who texted back. With whom nothing was ever a crisis. And who, somehow, felt a little flat.

It’s worth asking why the first feels like love and the second feels like a compromise, because the answer says more about how feelings work than about who was right for you.

A swaying bridge

In 1974 the psychologists Donald Dutton and Arthur Aron ran a now-famous study in British Columbia. An attractive female interviewer approached men as they crossed either a high, narrow suspension bridge that swayed over a deep canyon, or a low, solid wooden bridge. She asked them to fill in a short questionnaire and gave them her phone number in case they wanted to know more.

About half the men on the frightening bridge called her afterwards. On the solid bridge, about one in eight did. [Experiment: Dutton & Aron, 1974] The usual interpretation is that the men misread their racing hearts, caused by the bridge, as attraction to the woman.

The study is small and old, and later research on “misattribution of arousal” has been mixed, so it shouldn’t carry too much weight. But it captures something many people know from experience: the body doesn’t always label its own excitement accurately. Fear, uncertainty and novelty produce the same pounding heart as desire, and in the moment, it’s easy to call all of it love.

Why uncertainty feels like depth

Now apply that to relationships. Someone who is warm one day and distant the next, who might text or might not, who leaves you not quite sure where you stand: that produces a lot of arousal. Your attention locks on. You think about them constantly. You replay conversations. When they come back, the relief is enormous.

From the inside, that feels like the most significant connection of your life. From the outside, a lot of it is the nervous system responding to unpredictability. Behavioural psychologists have long noted that unpredictable rewards produce some of the most persistent behaviour there is. [Idea: intermittent reinforcement, from learning research; applied here by analogy]

Intensity, in other words, can be a measure of how uncertain you are, not how close you are.

Who’s most likely to mix them up

Attachment research offers one clue about who’s most drawn to this pattern. Researchers describe adult attachment along two dimensions: anxiety, a fear of rejection and abandonment, and avoidance, discomfort with closeness and dependence. [Theory: Brennan, Clark & Shaver, 1998; building on Hazan & Shaver, 1987]

People higher in attachment anxiety tend to be very alert to signs that a partner is pulling away, and to feel the push-pull of an uncertain relationship especially strongly. For someone who grew up with love that was real but unpredictable, a partner who is sometimes there and sometimes not can feel deeply, strangely familiar. Familiar is easily mistaken for right. [Moodius reading, drawing on attachment theory]

None of this is a diagnosis, and nobody is simply “an anxious type.” These are dials, not boxes. But if the relationships that grip you hardest all share the same uncertainty, the dial is worth noticing.

What intimacy is actually made of

If intensity is a rush, intimacy is something slower. The psychologists Harry Reis and Phillip Shaver described intimacy as a process: one person reveals something of themselves, the other responds in a way that leaves them feeling understood, valued and cared for, and over time, both come to trust that they can be known. [Theory: Reis & Shaver, 1988]

Arthur Aron, of the bridge study, later tested a way of building closeness on purpose. In a 1997 study, pairs of strangers took turns asking each other a series of questions that became gradually more personal, while other pairs asked each other small-talk questions for the same time. The pairs with the deepening questions reported feeling significantly closer. [Experiment: Aron et al., 1997] The published set is now known as the “36 questions.”

Notice what produced the closeness: not uncertainty, not drama, but gradual, reciprocal disclosure. Both people revealed a bit more, in turn, and responded to each other. That’s almost the opposite of the push-pull relationship, where one person is always guessing.

Fromm’s warning

Erich Fromm saw this more than half a century ago. In The Art of Loving he warned that people often take the intensity of an infatuation, being “crazy” about each other, as proof of the strength of their love, when it may only show how lonely they were before. For Fromm, real love was less about falling than about standing: an activity, practised over time. [Idea: Fromm, The Art of Loving, 1956; paraphrased]

That’s a harsher line than most people want to hear, and it isn’t always true. Some intense loves become deep ones. But it’s a useful question to hold: is this feeling about them, or about how much I needed someone?

When calm feels like boredom

For people used to intensity, a steady, kind partner can at first feel like something is missing. The nervous system, tuned to watch for the next withdrawal, has nothing to do. It reads safety as flatness.

Some people even find themselves picking fights, testing the other person, or losing interest just as things become secure, as if part of them needs the old uncertainty back to feel anything at all. If you recognise that, it isn’t a flaw in your character. It’s a pattern that once made sense.

That feeling often fades if you give it time. Calm can become the background in which real closeness grows: the kind where you’re known, not just wanted. [Moodius reading] It’s also fine to decide, after giving it a fair chance, that a relationship isn’t right. The point isn’t to choose the dullest person available. It’s to stop using butterflies as the main test.

Try this: The Two Columns (15 minutes)

  1. Think of the relationship (past or present) that felt most intense. In one column, list what made it intense.
  2. In a second column, list the moments in that relationship when you felt genuinely known: understood, accepted, safe to be ordinary.
  3. Compare the lengths. Be honest about which column is longer.
  4. Now do the same for one calmer relationship, romantic or not.
  5. Write one sentence about what you want more of: the first column or the second.

Three questions for your journal

  1. Which of my relationships felt most intense, and how much of that intensity came from not knowing where I stood?
  2. When someone is consistently kind to me, what do I feel, and what do I do?
  3. Who knows me best right now, and when did I last tell them something new?

Known, not just wanted

Intensity is real, and it isn’t worthless. But it’s a reading of how stirred up you are, and uncertainty is one of the fastest ways to stir people up. Intimacy is a different instrument. It measures whether you can be seen, slowly, by someone who stays.

The relationship that will matter most may not be the one that made your heart race on the bridge. It may be the one where, after the bridge, there’s somewhere solid to stand.

If a relationship involves control, threats, or fear of what your partner might do, that isn’t intensity. It’s a safety issue. In the UK, the National Domestic Abuse Helpline is free on 0808 2000 247, any hour; in an emergency call 999.
