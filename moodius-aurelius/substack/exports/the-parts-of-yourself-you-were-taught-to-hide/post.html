Somewhere around the age of five, most of us run a quiet experiment. We cry, or rage, or boast, or ask for too much, or say something too strange, and we watch the faces of the adults around us. Some reactions are warm. Some are cold. Some are laughter at our expense.

We learn fast. By the time we’re grown, we have a precise, unspoken map of which parts of us are welcome and which are not. The sensitive boy learns to make a joke instead. The angry girl learns to be helpful. The ambitious child in a modest family learns that wanting things out loud is showing off. The parts that weren’t welcome don’t go away. They go underground.

The persona and the rest

Carl Jung had a word for the face we learn to show: the persona, after the mask worn by actors in ancient theatre. He didn’t think it was dishonest. Everyone needs a way of meeting the world. The trouble, in his view, starts when we forget there is anything behind it, and the disowned parts collect in what he called the shadow. [Interpretive framework: Jung, Aion and related works; not a tested theory]

The paediatrician and psychoanalyst Donald Winnicott described something similar from the child’s side: a “false self” that develops to fit what the environment demands, protecting a “true self” that doesn’t feel safe to show. In his account, the false self is a reasonable adaptation, not a failure. It only becomes a problem when it’s all there is. [Idea: Winnicott, true and false self; paraphrased]

These are frameworks, not findings. But they describe something most people recognise. The question is what the hiding actually costs.

What hiding costs

Psychology has studied two kinds of hiding closely: hiding feelings, and hiding facts.

On feelings, James Gross and Oliver John compared people who habitually suppress the outward expression of emotion with people who habitually reappraise what’s happening. Habitual suppressors reported more negative emotion, less positive emotion, lower wellbeing and weaker closeness with others. [Study: Gross & John, 2003] The studies are correlational. But they fit a common experience: the person who never shows what they feel often ends up feeling it more, and more alone.

On facts, Michael Slepian and colleagues ran ten studies on secrets. Participants were currently keeping secrets in about 13 of the 38 categories researchers asked about, on average, and about five of those they had never told anyone. The surprising part came next. People spent far more time thinking about their secrets when they were alone, with their mind wandering, than actively hiding them in conversation. And it was the mind-wandering, not the hiding, that went with lower wellbeing. [Studies: Slepian, Chun & Mason, 2017]

That reframes the cost. Hiding a part of yourself isn’t mainly expensive at the dinner table. It’s expensive in the car on the way home, in the shower, at 3 a.m., when the hidden thing keeps coming back to be looked after.

The parts men are taught to hide

For many men, the map drawn in childhood has a well-known shape. Fear, tenderness, grief, need: the feelings that might look like weakness go first. The research on masculine norms suggests this isn’t harmless. In a set of meta-analyses, Joel Wong and colleagues found that conforming to traditional masculine norms was modestly linked to worse mental health and more strongly to not seeking help, with self-reliance among the most consistently harmful. [Meta-analysis: Wong et al., 2017]

The writer bell hooks argued that the costs land on men themselves: to fit the role, they’re asked to cut away whole parts of their emotional life, and then wonder why love feels hard. [Idea: hooks, The Will to Change, 2004; paraphrased] It isn’t only men. Women are often taught to hide anger, ambition and appetite. The specific parts differ. The mechanism is the same.

Shame is the lock

Why is it so hard to bring these parts back? Because they were usually hidden under shame, not guilt.

The distinction matters. Guilt says I did something bad and tends to push people towards repair. Shame says I am bad and tends to push people towards hiding, denial or anger. [Review: Tangney & Dearing, 2002] A child who was laughed at for crying doesn’t conclude “crying in that moment was unwise.” He concludes “the part of me that cries is unacceptable.” That conclusion can outlive the people who taught it by decades.

How to spot what went underground

Hidden parts tend to leave traces, and you can learn to read them. A few common ones: - Over-reaction to other people. The trait you can’t stand in a colleague is sometimes one you were punished for. (An earlier essay in this series looked at the research on that.) - A reflex that arrives too fast. The joke, the change of subject, the sudden helpfulness, appearing at exactly the moment a feeling would have. - Envy. People who openly have what you’ve hidden (confidence, softness, ambition, play) can stir something sharper than admiration. - The 3 a.m. return. If Slepian is right, what you’ve hidden comes back when the mind wanders. What you think about alone is a map of what you carry.

None of these proves anything on its own. They’re places to look. [Moodius reading]

What recovering a part looks like

None of this means you should express everything. Discretion is a skill, not a symptom. Choosing not to share a feeling at work is different from no longer knowing you have it.

Recovering a hidden part usually looks small. Noticing that you’ve made a joke at the exact moment you felt hurt. Admitting to one person that you want something you’ve been pretending not to want. Letting yourself be angry in a journal before deciding whether to say anything. The aim isn’t a dramatic unmasking. It’s getting back the full set of options, so that hiding becomes a choice again rather than a reflex. [Moodius reading]

Try this: The Unwelcome List (20 minutes)

  1. Finish this sentence five times: As a child, it wasn’t safe to be… (too loud, too sad, too clever, too needy, too angry, too soft).
  2. Next to each, write who taught you, and how. A look, a joke, a punishment, a silence.
  3. Next to that, write what you do now instead, the replacement behaviour.
  4. Circle one that you’d like back, even a little.
  5. This week, find one low-stakes moment to let it show, and write down what actually happened, compared with what you feared.

Three questions for your journal

  1. Which part of me did I learn to hide first, and what was I protecting myself from?
  2. What do I think about most when I’m alone that nobody else knows I think about?
  3. Who in my life now would actually welcome the part I hide?

Coming back up

The hidden parts were hidden for good reasons. At five, the approval of the people around you was everything, and you did what it took to keep it. That was intelligent, not weak.

But the conditions have changed. You’re no longer five, and the adults who drew the map are not the only people who’ll ever see you. The parts that went underground are still yours. Some of them are probably the best of you. They’ve been waiting, at some cost, for you to decide it’s safe to let them back.

If what you’ve hidden includes trauma, abuse or thoughts of harming yourself, please don’t unpack it alone. Talk to a GP or a therapist, or in the UK call Samaritans on 116 123, any hour.
