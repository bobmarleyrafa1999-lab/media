What Would You Do If Nobody Was Watching?

Plato asked it with a magic ring. Psychologists asked it with a picture of eyes above an honesty box. The answers are less flattering, and more useful, than the question usually suggests.

It’s one of the oldest questions in moral philosophy, and one of the most used in self-help: what would you do if nobody was watching? It’s usually asked as a test. The right answer is supposed to be “exactly the same as I do now.”

That’s a fine aspiration. But it isn’t usually the honest answer, and the question is more interesting, and more practical, if you let it be honest.

The ring

In Book II of Plato’s Republic, Glaucon tells the story of Gyges, a shepherd who finds a ring that makes him invisible. He uses it to seduce the queen, kill the king and take the throne. Glaucon’s point is blunt. Give the just man and the unjust man the same ring, and they’ll behave the same way:

“No man would keep his hands off what was not his own when he could safely take what he liked out of the market.” Plato, Republic II, tr. Benjamin Jowett

Anyone who had such power and still did no wrong, Glaucon adds, “would be thought by the lookers-on to be a most wretched idiot,” though they would praise him to each other’s faces. [Philosophy: Plato, Republic II, Jowett; partly quoted, rest paraphrased]

Glaucon doesn’t believe this himself. He’s stating the cynic’s case as strongly as he can so that Socrates has to answer it, and the rest of the Republic is the answer: justice is worth having for its own sake, because of what it does to the soul of the person who practises it. But the challenge has outlived most answers to it. Do we behave well because it’s right, or because we’re seen?

The eyes above the honesty box

In 2006 three researchers at Newcastle University tested a small version of Glaucon’s question. Their department’s coffee room ran on an honesty box: you took milk for your tea and put money in a tin. For ten weeks, a notice above the box carried a different image each week, alternating between a pair of eyes and a picture of flowers.

In the weeks with eyes, people paid about 2.76 times as much per litre of milk as in the weeks with flowers. [Experiment: Bateson, Nettle & Roberts, 2006] Nobody was actually watching. A photograph was enough.

It became one of the most cited findings in behavioural science, and inspired eye images on posters everywhere from bike sheds to supermarkets. Then the larger tests arrived. In 2017 a team led by Stefanie Northover pooled dozens of experiments on whether images of eyes make people more generous with money. Across 26 experiments with about 2,700 participants, and 27 experiments with nearly 20,000, they found no reliable effect. [Meta-analysis: Northover et al., 2017]

So the honest picture is mixed. Being watched, or feeling watched, probably does nudge some behaviour in some settings. But a picture of eyes doesn’t reliably turn people into better versions of themselves. Glaucon’s cynicism is too simple, and so is the headline that disproved it.

The more useful question

Here’s the turn. If you treat “what would you do if nobody was watching?” only as a test of honesty, it has one right answer and most people already know it. But if you treat it as a decision tool, it does something more valuable. It removes reputation from the calculation.

Take any choice you’re stuck on: the job, the move, the relationship, the thing you’ve always wanted to try. Ask what you’d do if nobody would ever know, if no colleague, parent, ex or follower would see the outcome or judge the choice. What’s left when you remove the audience is often what you actually want. [Moodius reading]

Try it on something small first. Would you still go to that dinner, keep that hobby, wear that, say yes to that, if no one would ever hear about it? You may be surprised how many choices are made for an imagined audience that isn’t even paying attention.

That’s not the same as ignoring other people. You still owe people things: honesty, care, keeping your word. The question strips out what they’ll think, not what you owe. Those are easy to confuse, and confusing them is how many people end up living for an audience.

The regrets nobody sees

Why does this matter? Because of what people regret.

In a series of studies in the 1990s, Thomas Gilovich and Victoria Medvec found that in the short term people tend to regret things they did, but looking back over their lives they tend to regret things they didn’t do. [Review: Gilovich & Medvec, 1995] Later work by Shai Davidai and Gilovich refined this. Across six studies, people’s regrets were mostly about their ideal selves, the hopes and aspirations they didn’t pursue (72 percent), rather than their ought selves, the duties they didn’t fulfil (28 percent). Asked for their single biggest regret, 76 percent named an ideal-self one. [Studies: Davidai & Gilovich, 2018]

Their explanation is telling. When we fail a duty, we usually do something about it: apologise, repair, make up for it. When we fail an aspiration, nothing forces us to act, so it lingers. And the ideal-self choices are exactly the ones most shaped by the audience: the novel you didn’t write because it might be bad, the career you didn’t try because it would look strange, the person you didn’t ask because they might say no in front of people.

So “what would you do if nobody was watching?” isn’t only a test of whether you’d steal milk money. It’s also a way of finding the choices you’re most likely to regret not making.

Two readings, both true

There are two honest answers to the title question, and they pull in different directions.

The first is Plato’s. If you’d behave worse unwatched, that’s worth knowing, and worth working on, because character is what you are when the ring is on.

The second is the one most people never consider. If you’d live differently unwatched, braver, stranger, more yourself, that’s worth knowing too. It may be the clearest signal you’ll get about which choices you’ll later wish you’d made.

Try this: The Invisible Decision (15 minutes)

  1. Write down one decision you’ve been putting off.
  2. Write what you’d choose if every person you know would approve.
  3. Write what you’d choose if every person you know would disapprove.
  4. Now write what you’d choose if nobody would ever know either way.
  5. Compare the three. Separate what you owe people (keep that) from what you fear they’ll think (question that). Write one next step.

Three questions for your journal

  1. What would I do differently tomorrow if nobody I know would ever find out?
  2. Which choices am I making for an audience, and who exactly is in it?
  3. What hope have I left unpursued that I’m most likely to regret at seventy?

Taking off the ring

Plato’s question is usually used to expose people. Used well, it can also free them. Take away the audience, and you see two things clearly: where your behaviour depends on being watched, and where your life does.

The first is a matter of character. The second is a matter of courage. Both are worth knowing, and both are easier to see with the ring on.
